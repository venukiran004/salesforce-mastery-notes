CRM Analytics — 100 Scenarios

Recipes and joins that silently change your numbers, security predicates, bindings, SAQL, Einstein Discovery model operations, consumption, and choosing the right tool in the modern stack.

📚
How to use this Answer out loud in ninety seconds, then open the answer. Concepts on CRM Analytics and its Advanced section.

Data prep (1–20)

1 Your dataset total does not match a Salesforce report.Hard

Nearly always a join in the recipe: an inner join silently dropping rows with no match, or a one-to-many join multiplying rows so every subsequent sum is inflated.

Check the row count after every join node, and reconcile the dataset total against a report before anyone builds a dashboard.

2 Rows disappeared after a join.Easy

An inner join dropped records with no match on the key. Switch to a left join if the left-hand rows must survive.

The node preview shows the count before and after — which is why previewing at each step beats debugging the output.

3 Revenue is suddenly three times too high.Hard

A one-to-many join multiplied the parent rows — each opportunity now appears once per line item, so summing the opportunity amount triples it.

Aggregate the child side before joining, or sum the child's value rather than the parent's.

4 Recipe or dataflow for new work?Easy

Recipe (Data Prep) — visual, with a live preview at each node, more capability, and where Salesforce is investing.

Dataflows are maintenance-only now; migrate when you next need to touch one.

5 A recipe takes two hours to run.Medium

Usually unnecessary volume: filtering late rather than early, joining before aggregating, or ingesting fields nobody uses.

Filter and trim as early as possible in the node chain, and aggregate before joining where the grain allows.

6 How often should a recipe run?Medium

From the decision latency the business genuinely needs — not "hourly just in case".

Recipe runtime and row ingestion both meter, so an hourly refresh of a 200-million-row dataset is a budget conversation waiting to happen.

7 A field is the wrong type in the dataset.Easy

Fix it in the recipe with an explicit transform rather than working around it in every dashboard query.

Numeric fields arriving as text is the common case, and it breaks aggregation silently.

8 You need data from an external system.Medium

A connector, an external data upload, or — preferably if it already lives in a warehouse — Data Cloud zero-copy rather than ingesting it twice.

Ingesting a copy means you now own its freshness, its volume and its reconciliation.

9 Dates are not grouping by fiscal period.Medium

Date fields need the fiscal settings applied during data prep, and the dataset must carry the fiscal attributes you want to group by.

Fiscal grouping is a data-prep decision, not a dashboard one.

10 A dataset is approaching its row limit.Medium

Filter to the rows that are actually analysed (a date window), pre-aggregate to a coarser grain, or split by period.

Most datasets at the limit contain years of detail nobody queries at row level.

11 You need a derived metric in every dashboard.Easy

Compute it once in the recipe so every query reads a plain field — dashboards should read, not calculate.

Repeating the same derivation in twelve queries guarantees they drift apart.

12 A recipe failed overnight.Medium

Check the run history for the failing node — commonly a source field that was renamed or removed, or a connector credential that expired.

Alert on failure and on absence, because a recipe that silently stops leaves yesterday's data looking current.

13 The dataset is stale and nobody noticed.Medium

Show the last-refreshed timestamp on every dashboard. Users cannot assess staleness they cannot see.

That single addition prevents most "the numbers are wrong" conversations, which are frequently "the numbers are from Tuesday".

14 You need row-level detail and aggregates.Medium

Two datasets at different grains, or one detailed dataset with the dashboard aggregating — depending on volume and query cost.

Pre-aggregating is usually faster; keeping detail is more flexible. Decide deliberately rather than keeping both.

15 A recipe joins on a key that is not unique.Hard

That is the row-multiplication problem in disguise. Verify key uniqueness before joining — or aggregate the many-side first.

Non-unique join keys are the most common cause of inflated numbers that look plausible for months.

16 Nulls are breaking your calculations.Easy

Handle them explicitly in the recipe with a default or an exclusion, rather than letting each query decide.

Null handling scattered across dashboards produces inconsistent totals from the same dataset.

17 You need to track changes over time.Medium

Append a dated snapshot in the recipe so each run adds a period's rows — which is how you get genuine trending rather than current state.

Plan the volume: a daily snapshot grows quickly, so choose the grain carefully.

18 A transformation is complex and undocumented.Medium

Name the nodes descriptively and document the recipe's purpose and key decisions — a recipe is business logic, and it will be inherited by someone.

An unnamed twenty-node recipe is as unmaintainable as any undocumented code.

19 How do you test a recipe change?Medium

Preview node by node, then compare the output dataset's row count and key totals against the previous version before publishing.

A golden set of expected totals is the only reliable regression test for data prep.

20 What is the single most common data-prep mistake?Hard

Joining without checking the row count, so an inner join loses rows or a one-to-many join multiplies them.

Both produce confidently wrong dashboards. Checking counts after every join is the cheapest quality control available.

Security (21–38)

21 Everyone can see everyone's data in a dashboard.Hard

No row-level security on the dataset. CRM Analytics does not inherit Salesforce sharing automatically.

You need sharing inheritance (for supported objects, within a row-count ceiling) or a security predicate you write yourself.

22 What is a security predicate?Medium

A row-level filter expression evaluated per user — for example 'OwnerId' == "$User.Id".

It is entirely your responsibility: a predicate matching nothing shows an empty dashboard, and one matching everything leaks data with no error at all.

23 A predicate shows nothing for everyone.Medium

Field name, case, or type mismatch — predicates fail closed and silently.

Test as several personas with known expected row counts, which is the only way to tell "correctly restrictive" from "broken".

24 Managers need to see their team's rows.Hard

Flatten the role hierarchy in the recipe into a list you can reference in the predicate, so a manager's roll-up is expressible.

And re-run the flatten when the hierarchy changes, or the predicate silently becomes stale.

25 Sharing inheritance is enabled but access is wrong.Hard

It is supported for a limited set of objects and has a row-count ceiling — beyond it, access falls back to something else.

You must know what the fallback is for your dataset, because that is the behaviour users will actually get.

26 App sharing versus row-level security.Medium

App sharing controls who can open the app, dashboards and datasets. Row-level security controls which rows they see.

Both are needed: app access without a predicate is a data leak, and a predicate without app access is pointless.

27 How do you test a predicate properly?Medium

A test matrix of personas — a rep, a manager, a regional lead, an exec — with expected row counts, verified before release and again after any hierarchy change.

"It looked right as an admin" is how analytics data leaks, and the leak is invisible until someone notices.

28 A dataset contains data some viewers must never see.Hard

Prefer excluding it at the recipe stage for those populations — a separate dataset — over relying on a predicate for highly sensitive data.

Defence in depth: the safest row is the one that was never ingested.

29 Does CRM Analytics respect field-level security?Medium

Not automatically at query time the way reports do — the dataset contains whatever the recipe ingested, for anyone with access.

So FLS must be enforced by what you choose to ingest and by dataset-level access, not assumed.

30 A predicate is slowing queries down.Medium

It is evaluated per query, so a complex expression over a large dataset costs real time.

Simplify by pre-computing the comparison values into dataset fields during data prep.

31 You need different security for two dashboards.Medium

Security lives on the dataset, not the dashboard — so different rules mean different datasets.

That is a design consequence worth knowing before building one dataset for every audience.

32 An auditor asks who can see what.Medium

Produce the app sharing settings, the dataset predicates, and the persona test matrix with expected row counts.

That evidence is only available if the test matrix exists, which is an argument for creating it during build.

33 A new hire sees no data.Easy

Their user attributes do not satisfy the predicate — frequently a missing role, a blank custom field, or a hierarchy flatten that has not re-run.

Predicates depend on user data being correct, which makes user provisioning part of your analytics security.

34 Should predicates reference custom user fields?Medium

They can, and it is often the cleanest way to express a business rule — but it makes that field load-bearing for security.

Document it, and validate it is populated for every user who needs access.

35 External users need a dashboard.Hard

Possible with the right licensing and embedding, and the row-level security becomes critical — an over-broad predicate exposes one customer's data to another.

Test as the actual external persona, and prefer separate datasets per population for anything sensitive.

36 A predicate was changed and nobody tested it.Hard

That is a potential data-exposure change with no error to signal it.

Treat predicate changes like security changes: reviewed, with the persona matrix re-run before release.

37 How do you handle sensitive fields in a shared dataset?Medium

Do not ingest them, or mask or bucket them during data prep — salary bands rather than salaries.

Bucketing frequently satisfies the analytical need without carrying the sensitive value.

38 What is the biggest analytics security risk?Hard

A dataset with no predicate, or an incorrect one — because it fails silently in the permissive direction and nobody reports seeing too much.

Which is why the persona test matrix is not optional for anything beyond your own data.

Dashboards & bindings (39–60)

39 Clicking one chart should filter another.Medium

A selection binding — one widget's query depends on what the user selected in another. That is the basis of drill-down.

Faceting handles the simple same-dataset case automatically; bindings are for everything else.

40 A total changed unexpectedly when a user clicked something.Medium

Automatic faceting — widgets on the same dataset cross-filter each other by default.

Turn it off deliberately for widgets that should stay global, which is a frequent source of "why did my grand total change".

41 You need to compute a cohort then filter by it.Hard

A results binding — one query consumes another query's results, so step one defines the cohort and step two filters by it.

That is the pattern for most genuinely analytical dashboard requirements.

42 Binding syntax is hard to get right.Medium

{{cell(stepName.selection, 0, "field").asString()}} and its relatives — verbose, and worth learning properly because it unlocks most real requirements.

Build and test one binding at a time; debugging three simultaneously is unproductive.

43 A filter should offer a fixed list of options.Easy

A static step with the values you choose, rather than a dynamic step querying live values.

Static steps also avoid offering options that return nothing, which looks like a bug.

44 A dashboard is slow to load.Medium

Too many steps, expensive queries, or calculation that should have happened in the recipe.

Pre-compute in data prep so dashboards read rather than calculate — that is the main lever for dashboard performance.

45 You need a period-over-period comparison.Medium

A compare table handles many of these declaratively — try it before writing SAQL.

For moving averages and running totals you will need SAQL windowing, which is the usual reason to drop down a level.

46 When do you actually need SAQL?Hard

Windowing (moving averages, running totals), custom cohort logic, and anything the builder cannot express.

Compact form is what the builder generates and is sufficient for most dashboards — SAQL is a capability, not a default style.

47 A SAQL query is unmaintainable.Medium

Push the complexity into the recipe and keep the query simple — dataset design beats query cleverness consistently.

A dashboard whose logic lives in hand-written SAQL is one nobody else can modify.

48 Users want to export dashboard data.Easy

Supported, with limits on row counts — and it is worth asking why, since heavy export usually signals a missing report or integration.

It is also a security surface worth being aware of.

49 A dashboard must be embedded in a record page.Medium

Supported, filtered by the record's context — which is one of the strongest arguments for CRM Analytics over an external BI tool.

Watch page performance: an embedded dashboard runs its queries on page load.

50 Mobile users need the dashboard.Medium

Design for it explicitly — a desktop dashboard with twelve widgets is unusable on a phone.

Fewer widgets, larger targets, and the one number they actually need at the top.

51 How many widgets should a dashboard have?Medium

As many as the decisions it supports — usually far fewer than people request.

Each widget is a query. Twenty widgets is both slow and unreadable, and the unreadability is the bigger problem.

52 A dashboard shows different numbers from a report.Hard

Join behaviour in the recipe, a security predicate filtering the viewer's rows, a staleness gap, or a different filter definition.

Check the recipe joins and the refresh time first — those two account for most discrepancies.

53 You need a dashboard for 500 users.Medium

Query concurrency and consumption both matter at that scale, so keep the queries cheap and pre-aggregate aggressively.

Also verify the security predicate performs acceptably per user, since it is evaluated on every query.

54 Nobody uses the dashboard.Medium

It probably does not answer a question they have, or it is not where they work.

Embed it in their path, and pair each number with the decision it supports — an unused dashboard is a requirement nobody actually had.

55 How do you version a dashboard?Medium

CRM Analytics assets are metadata and deploy — but the datasets they depend on must exist in the target, which is the fiddly part.

Keep the recipe and dashboard definitions in version control and promote them together.

56 A dashboard deployed but shows no data.Medium

The dataset does not exist in the target org, or the recipe has not run there yet, or the security predicate fails for the target org's users.

Promote the data pipeline before the presentation layer.

57 Users want to build their own.Medium

Encourage it on well-designed datasets — self-service scales and a build queue does not.

Your effort belongs in the datasets, the metric definitions and the security, which are the things they cannot get right themselves.

58 A metric needs the same definition everywhere.Medium

Compute it in the recipe as a field, so every dashboard reads the same value.

Defining it per dashboard produces the "two dashboards, two revenues" conversation reliably.

59 A dashboard needs data from two datasets.Medium

Possible with separate steps and bindings, but faceting will not work across datasets automatically.

If the widgets must interact, joining into one dataset in the recipe is usually simpler.

60 How do you make a dashboard trustworthy?Medium

A visible last-refreshed timestamp, documented metric definitions, reconciled totals against a known source, and a tested security predicate.

Trust comes from provenance. Polish without provenance produces confident wrong decisions.

Einstein Discovery (61–80)

61 A model predicts churn beautifully and is useless.Hard

Leakage — it was trained on a field only populated after the outcome, such as the cancellation reason.

Remove any field that would not be known at prediction time. A model that looks too good almost always is.

62 Accuracy is 98% and the model is worthless.Hard

Class imbalance: with a 2% event rate, "always predict no" scores 98%.

Judge on precision and recall for the rare class, never raw accuracy — this is the most common misreading of a model's quality.

63 How do you define the outcome variable?Medium

Precisely, with the business. "Won" and "won within 90 days" are different models with different meanings and different usefulness.

An imprecise outcome definition produces a model nobody can act on.

64 There is not much historical data.Medium

Scoring needs a meaningful volume of closed outcomes. Below that, the model is noise with a confidence interval.

Be honest about it rather than shipping a model that will be trusted and wrong.

65 Bias checks flag a variable.Hard

Review it, and review the proxies too — postcode, job title and tenure can encode protected characteristics even when the protected field is absent.

Removing the obvious field while keeping its proxy is a common and serious mistake.

66 A model's quality has degraded.Medium

Drift — pricing changes, market shifts and process changes all invalidate a model quietly.

Schedule refreshes and compare predicted versus actual. A model nobody re-trains is a model nobody should trust.

67 Sales ignore the score.Medium

A number without a reason gets ignored. Surface the top contributing factors alongside it.

And tie it to a decision: a score that does not change what anyone does is pure cost.

68 Where should predictions be written?Medium

Onto the record, so they are usable in reports, list views, automation and Apex — and visible where the decision is made.

A prediction visible only inside an analytics dashboard reaches far fewer decisions.

69 How do you measure whether a model helped?Hard

Measure the decision, not the model: did the outcome improve for the population where the score changed behaviour?

Model accuracy metrics are necessary and insufficient. Business impact needs a baseline and ideally a holdout group.

70 Prediction Builder or Einstein Discovery?Medium

Prediction Builder for a simple field prediction configured by an admin. Discovery for richer analysis, explanation, bias checks and deployment options.

Start with the simplest tool that answers the question.

71 Should you bring your own model?Hard

If the team has genuine data-science capability and the problem warrants it, model hosting and BYOM options exist.

But you then own training, monitoring, drift and governance — which is a standing commitment, not a project.

72 A model needs to explain itself to a regulator.Hard

Discovery provides contributing factors, which helps — but "explainable" in a regulatory sense is a higher bar than "shows top factors".

Involve compliance early, and prefer a simpler, more interpretable model where explanation is a requirement.

73 How often should you re-train?Medium

On a schedule informed by how fast the business changes, plus whenever predicted-versus-actual diverges.

Set the schedule when you deploy; "we will re-train when it gets worse" means nobody is watching.

74 Predictions appear for records where they make no sense.Medium

The scoring scope is too broad — scoring records outside the training population produces meaningless numbers.

Scope predictions to the segment the model was trained on, and suppress them elsewhere.

75 The model's data is poor quality.Medium

Then the model is poor quality, confidently. Data work comes first — it is the highest-value AI activity and the one most often skipped.

A model on bad data produces fluent, wrong guidance that people follow.

76 How do you pilot a model safely?Medium

A limited population, a measured baseline, a human in the loop, and a defined success criterion before you start.

Then widen only if the data supports it. Launching to everyone and watching is not a pilot.

77 A stakeholder wants AI on day one of an implementation.Hard

There is no history to train on, no clean data, and no measured baseline — so there is nothing to learn from and nothing to prove improvement against.

Deliver the core process, instrument it, clean the data, then add models. Saying that costs enthusiasm and saves a failed pilot.

78 Predictions need to drive automation.Medium

Possible — the score is on the record and usable in flows and Apex — but put a human in the loop for anything consequential.

A model acting unsupervised on an irreversible decision is a risk nobody signed off for.

79 Two models disagree about the same record.Medium

They were trained on different outcomes or populations, which makes disagreement expected rather than contradictory.

Name each model's question explicitly on the page, or users will treat the numbers as competing.

80 What is the most common modelling mistake?Hard

Leakage, followed by judging on raw accuracy with an imbalanced class, and then deploying a score nobody acts on.

All three produce something that looks successful and changes nothing.

Tool choice, cost & judgement (81–100)

81 Reports or CRM Analytics?Medium

Reports for operational, live, single-object-with-children analysis — free, buildable by anyone, far cheaper to maintain.

CRM Analytics when you must blend sources Salesforce cannot join, trend beyond snapshots, or handle multi-million-row datasets.

82 CRM Analytics or Tableau?Medium

CRM Analytics when the audience works inside Salesforce and the data is mostly Salesforce — embedding is its real advantage.

Tableau for enterprise BI across many non-Salesforce sources and deeper visual analysis.

83 Data Cloud or CRM Analytics?Hard

Different jobs. Data Cloud unifies and resolves identity across systems and activates segments; CRM Analytics analyses and visualises.

They compose: Data Cloud as the unified source, CRM Analytics as the analysis layer.

84 The data already lives in a warehouse.Medium

Prefer zero copy over ingesting it again — you avoid duplication, credit consumption and a second freshness problem.

Ingesting a copy means you own its reconciliation forever.

85 Consumption costs are rising unexpectedly.Medium

Rows ingested, recipe runtime, query concurrency and Data Cloud credits all meter.

The usual cause is a frequent refresh of a large dataset "just in case" — set the cadence from the decision latency instead.

86 How do you reduce consumption?Medium

Ingest fewer rows and fewer fields, refresh less often, pre-aggregate, and delete datasets nobody queries.

Also check for recipes running on schedules nobody owns — they are a common quiet cost.

87 A stakeholder wants real-time analytics.Medium

Ask what decision depends on sub-hourly freshness. Usually the answer reveals that daily is fine, or that the decision belongs in an operational report rather than analytics.

Real-time analytics is expensive and frequently unexamined.

88 Who should own CRM Analytics?Medium

A named owner for the datasets, security predicates, metric definitions and consumption — the presentation layer can be more distributed.

Unowned analytics produces contradictory dashboards and surprise bills.

89 Nobody trusts the analytics.Medium

Usually one bad experience: a number that did not reconcile, or stale data presented as current.

Fix provenance — reconcile to a known source, show refresh times, document definitions — and trust returns slowly. It is much harder to rebuild than to establish.

90 How do you reconcile analytics to the source?Medium

Compare dataset row counts and key totals against a Salesforce report for the same scope and period, as a repeatable check.

Do it at build time and after every recipe change — that is your regression test.

91 An analytics project is six months in with no output.Medium

Usually trying to model everything before delivering anything. Ship one dataset answering one decision, then extend.

A perfect unified model nobody has used is worth less than one reconciled dashboard someone opens.

92 How do you prioritise what to build?Medium

By the decisions that would change: which recurring decision is currently made without data, and what would it take to inform it?

Starting from available data rather than needed decisions produces dashboards nobody requested.

93 A dataset duplicates one that already exists.Medium

Consolidate — duplicate datasets with slightly different logic are how two dashboards come to disagree.

Maintain a register of datasets with their purpose and owner, and review it periodically.

94 Users keep exporting to spreadsheets.Medium

The clearest signal that the analytics is not meeting the need — usually a missing dimension, a missing grain, or an inability to filter.

Ask what they do in the spreadsheet; that is the requirement you missed.

95 How do you document an analytics estate?Medium

Dataset purpose, source, refresh cadence, owner, security predicate, and the metric definitions it supports.

Without that, nobody can safely change a recipe, and nobody can answer where a number came from.

96 An analytics change broke a dashboard.Medium

A recipe field renamed or removed, or a grain change — dashboards depend on dataset structure.

Treat dataset schema as a contract: additive changes are safe, removals and renames need consumer checks.

97 Should every metric live in analytics?Medium

No — operational metrics belong in reports where they are live and free, and on the record where the decision happens.

Reserve analytics for what reports genuinely cannot do; otherwise you have built an expensive second reporting layer.

98 How do you hand over an analytics estate?Medium

The dataset register, the recipes in version control, the security test matrix, the metric definitions, and the consumption picture.

The security matrix is the part most often missing and the most expensive to reconstruct.

99 What is the most common CRM Analytics mistake?Hard

Unchecked joins producing wrong numbers, followed by an untested security predicate, and then computing in dashboards what should have been computed in the recipe.

The first two are correctness failures that look fine for months.

100 One habit that most improves analytics work?Hard

Checking the row count after every join, and reconciling the dataset total against a known report before anything is built on it.

Almost every wrong analytics number traces back to one of those two checks not happening, and both take seconds.
