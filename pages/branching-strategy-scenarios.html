Branching Strategy — 100 Scenarios

Branch-to-org mapping, hotfixes and forward-merges, the metadata that will not merge, config drift, PR gates, delta deployments, feature flags and parallel release trains.

📚
How to use this Answer out loud in ninety seconds, then open the answer. Concepts on Branching Strategy and Deployment & DevOps.

Models & mapping (1–18)

1 Why can't you use GitFlow exactly as written?Medium

Three platform realities: branches cannot be built in isolation (code only compiles and tests inside an org), changes arrive outside git (admins click-build in orgs), and some metadata will not merge.

The topology survives; the practice needs retrieval, drift detection and ignore rules a normal codebase never needs.

2 What is the single most important invariant?Easy

main equals production. Nothing else is deployed there, every release is a tagged commit on it, and nobody pushes directly.

That one rule is what makes hotfixes safe, rollback meaningful and audit questions answerable.

3 Someone asks whether main is what's live.Easy

If that question needs asking, the strategy has already failed — and every tag, rollback and audit answer is now a guess.

Restore the invariant first: baseline production into main, then enforce it with branch protection and drift detection.

4 Which model for a team of mostly admins?Medium

Org branching or simple GitFlow — and optimise for retrieval and drift detection rather than merge elegance.

The branch is a record of what changed, not a development workspace. The PR exists for the review conversation and the audit trail.

5 The business needs a frozen UAT scope.Easy

You need release branches. Cut from develop, freeze the scope, and develop reopens immediately for the next cycle.

That single requirement rules out trunk-based for most enterprises, and it is close to universal.

6 When is trunk-based actually viable?Hard

When the team is mostly developers with real test coverage, you have working feature flags so incomplete work merges switched off, and the business does not require a frozen signed-off scope.

Package-based development makes it far more practical. Without flags you just get long-lived branches with a trunk-based label.

7 Map branches to orgs for a four-tier setup.Easy

feature/* → scratch org or dev sandbox; develop → integration sandbox; release/* → UAT; main → production.

Long-lived branches map to persistent orgs; short-lived branches map to disposable ones.

8 Your branch topology exceeds your org topology.Medium

Then some branches have nowhere to be validated, which makes their gates theatre.

Either buy the environment or simplify the topology. One Full sandbox refreshed every 29 days genuinely constrains what branch model is honest.

9 What does a branch mean in your model?Medium

Exactly one thing: either an org's current state or a unit of work. Never both.

"develop is the dev org, but also where features queue, but also what we release from" is the root of most branching confusion.

10 Package-based branching — when?Medium

Once you have clean modular boundaries, dependency discipline and strong coverage. It genuinely solves drift and cross-team contention.

Get org-based GitFlow boring first. Doing both migrations at once stalls programmes for a year.

11 How long should a feature branch live?Easy

Days. If it is older than a sprint, that is the problem to fix.

Metadata conflicts grow superlinearly with branch age because so much of it lives in shared files — a six-week branch is where merges go to die.

12 What should a branch name contain?Easy

Type, ticket id and a short slug: feature/ABC-123-quote-rounding.

The ticket id is how a deployment is traced to a requirement eighteen months later, and it lets release notes be generated automatically.

13 Do you need a develop branch?Medium

If you release on a cadence and need an integration point, yes. If you release continuously behind flags, main alone is simpler.

An unused develop that drifts from main is worse than not having one.

14 A long programme runs alongside BAU.Hard

A long-lived project/* branch with its own org and a mandatory scheduled merge from develop — weekly minimum, in the sprint plan, with a named owner.

Without that cadence, re-integration becomes its own project with its own budget. The structural alternative is unlocked packages.

15 Is a branch per developer a good idea?Easy

No — integration then never happens until the end, which is the problem branching was meant to solve.

Branch per unit of work, merged within days.

16 How do you handle a spike or experiment?Easy

A spike/* branch that is never merged, and is deleted.

Letting an experiment become a feature by accident is how untested, undesigned code reaches production.

17 Where do config retrievals go?Medium

A config/* branch — retrieved admin changes, PR-ed into main like any other change, then merged down.

That gives production changes a review trail without pretending they came from development.

18 How do you pick a model in an interview answer?Hard

Answer five questions first: does the business need a frozen scope; who makes changes; how often must you ship; how good is coverage honestly; how many sandboxes do you have.

The model falls out of those. Naming GitFlow before asking them is reciting, not designing.

Hotfixes & releases (19–38)

19 You branched a hotfix from develop.Easy

You are about to ship every unreleased feature in develop alongside your fix.

Hotfixes branch from main, always, because main equals production.

20 Walk through a hotfix end to end.Medium

Branch from main; fix with a test that fails without the fix; validate against production with specified tests; deploy (quick deploy of the validated build); tag it.

Then forward-merge main into develop and every open release/* — the step people skip.

21 You skipped the forward-merge.Hard

The next release deploys the old broken code over your fix, and the defect reappears having apparently been fixed.

Automate a CI check that fails whenever main contains commits absent from develop or any open release branch. Discipline alone does not survive a busy month.

22 A hotfix has no test. Is that acceptable?Medium

No — a hotfix without a failing-then-passing test is how the same defect ships twice.

It also makes the deployment harder, because RunSpecifiedTests needs tests covering the changed class.

23 How do you cut a release?Easy

Create release/2026.04 from develop. From that moment its scope is frozen, and develop reopens immediately for the next cycle.

Only in-scope bug fixes land on the release branch, and each also merges back to develop.

24 A bug is found in UAT.Easy

bugfix/* off the release branch, merged back to it — and immediately merged into develop too.

Fixing it on develop means it misses the release; fixing it only on the release branch means it is lost next cycle.

25 How do you close a release?Easy

Merge to main, deploy (ideally quick deploy of the validated build), tag v2026.04.0, merge main back into develop, delete the release branch.

Keep the tag forever — it is your rollback reference and your audit record.

26 Why tag every release?Easy

It is your only real rollback story on a platform with no transactional rollback: "deploy the previous tag" plus a documented list of what cannot be redeployed.

It also makes "what shipped when" answerable in seconds.

27 Cherry-picking is how you assemble releases.Hard

Then you ship commits that were never tested together, and history becomes a fiction.

If you need it routinely, the branch model is wrong: use release branches (choose what merges) or feature flags (ship it off).

28 A feature must be pulled from the release at the last minute.Hard

If it is behind a flag, switch it off — trivial. If it is not, you are reverting commits from a tested branch and re-testing, which is genuinely risky.

That asymmetry is the strongest practical argument for feature flags.

29 Two release branches are live at once.Hard

Normal with two cadences, and the place discipline breaks: both must receive every hotfix and every merge to main.

Automate the staleness check. Relying on someone remembering two forward-merges is how a fix is lost silently.

30 How long should a release branch live?Medium

One cycle, then deleted. A long-lived release branch re-introduces exactly the drift that release branches were meant to contain.

If stabilisation routinely takes weeks, the problem is upstream quality.

31 What is a release dry run?Medium

A validation-only deployment of the delta against production, with the real test level — plus a rehearsal of any data or config-data migration.

It converts release night from a discovery exercise into an execution of something already proven.

32 Production changed between validation and deployment.Medium

Quick deploy becomes ineligible, and you must re-validate.

Which is a reason to lock down production edit rights during a release window, and to keep the validate-to-deploy gap short.

33 How do you generate release notes?Easy

From the ticket ids in branch names and merge commits between the previous tag and this one.

Hand-written notes omit what nobody wanted to mention, which is frequently what operations needs to know.

34 A release needs to be rolled back.Hard

Deploy the previous tag, then manually reverse what cannot be redeployed: deletions, data changes, picklist values, irreversible settings.

That per-release irreversible list is what turns the rollback decision from a guess into a judgement — and it must be written beforehand.

35 Fix forward or roll back?Medium

Fix forward when the fix is small and understood; roll back when the change is broad and the previous state is known-good.

On this platform forward-fix is usually faster, which is why a fast hotfix path matters more than an elaborate rollback plan.

36 A release is blocked by one unfinished feature.Medium

That is what the freeze is for: it should not have been merged into develop before the cut, or it should be behind a flag.

Ship the release without it. Holding a whole release for one item is how cadences collapse.

37 How do you handle an emergency outside the release process?Medium

Through the sanctioned hotfix path, which should be fast enough that bypassing it is never tempting — tens of minutes, not hours.

If "urgent" is used weekly to bypass review, that is a process problem, not an emergency pattern.

38 What does a healthy release cadence look like?Medium

Predictable, with a frozen scope, a dry run, a short window, a tag, and forward-merges completed the same day.

Frequency matters less than predictability — a reliable fortnightly train beats an unpredictable weekly one.

The metadata that will not merge (39–58)

39 Git cleanly merged two edits to the same flow.Hard

That is the dangerous outcome. A flow is one XML blob with generated element coordinates — a line-by-line merge produces a flow that deploys and behaves incorrectly.

Mark flow files unmergeable in .gitattributes so a conflict is forced, and take one side wholesale.

40 Two people must change the same flow.Medium

Sequentially: the second rebases and reapplies their change in the builder.

Structurally: one owner per flow per release, and split large flows into subflows so two streams of work are not in the same file.

41 Profiles conflict on every pull request.Medium

A profile is one enormous XML file covering every object, field and tab, so unrelated features both rewrite it.

Stop versioning them: move permissions to permission sets and groups, strip profiles to the minimum, and .forceignore them.

42 What is the risk of ignoring profiles?Medium

They become an unmanaged surface — changed per org with no review trail.

So the trade must be explicit: documented as manually managed, with Setup Audit Trail review covering them. Ignoring them silently is worse than the conflicts.

43 Page layouts conflict constantly.Medium

Field order is positional, so two additions collide on the same lines.

Prefer Dynamic Forms (which removes most layout churn), assign layout ownership per team, and expect manual resolution for the rest.

44 Custom labels conflict on every PR.Easy

All labels live in one file. Treat it as append-only with alphabetical ordering, and configure a union merge driver so both sides' additions are kept.

Same approach for translation files.

45 Someone reordered a global value set.Medium

Rewrites the whole file and conflicts with everyone. Treat value sets as append-only and never reorder.

Review these changes carefully — a removed value is a breaking change for records already using it.

46 package.xml conflicts in every PR.Easy

Stop hand-maintaining it. Generate the manifest from the git diff at deployment time.

A hand-maintained manifest also guarantees omissions, which is a second reason to generate it.

47 How do you force a conflict on an unmergeable file?Medium

.gitattributes with a merge strategy that refuses to auto-merge (or -diff to stop git treating it as text).

A forced conflict is the desired outcome: a human then decides which version wins.

48 Record types and picklist assignments are scattered.Medium

They live in object XML and profile XML simultaneously, which is why they conflict in two places.

Source-format decomposition helps; moving permissions to permission sets helps more.

49 CPQ configuration does not appear in git at all.Hard

Because it is data, not metadata — price rules, conditions, actions, lookup queries, product options.

You need a scripted, version-controlled migration in dependency order, with references re-pointed by name. Same for Field Service policies and territories.

50 How do you review a flow change in a PR?Hard

Not from the XML diff, which is unreadable. Open it in the builder in a sandbox against a written description of the intended change.

Check entry conditions, running context, fault paths, loops containing DML or Apex, and whether a Flow Test exists.

51 What should .forceignore contain?Medium

Typically profiles, org-level settings, and unfiled public reports — the metadata that is hostile to version control or org-specific by nature.

Document every entry and who manages it manually, or you have created invisible drift.

52 A merge resolved cleanly but the deployment fails.Medium

Often a semantic conflict: both sides were valid individually and the combination is not — a field referenced by one and deleted by the other.

Which is why the delta validation on develop matters; clean merges are not correct merges.

53 Dashboards and reports conflict.Medium

They are metadata but awkward: folder structure and sharing are the fiddly parts, and generated layout XML churns.

Many teams ignore unfiled public reports entirely and manage dashboards per org.

54 What is the root cause of most merge pain?Hard

Branch lifetime. Metadata conflicts grow superlinearly with age because so much metadata lives in large shared files.

One story per branch, merged within days, prevents more pain than any tooling you can buy.

55 Two teams own the same object's automation.Medium

Assign explicit ownership of conflict-prone components — one owner per flow, per layout, per global value set — and a daily merge-down habit.

Shared ownership of a single XML file is a guaranteed recurring conflict.

56 How do you reduce the number of conflict-prone files?Medium

Dynamic Forms instead of many layouts; permission sets instead of profiles; subflows instead of monolithic flows; generated manifests instead of a hand-maintained one.

Each change removes a whole class of recurring conflict.

57 Someone resolved a conflict by taking both sides of a flow.Hard

That is a corrupt flow that will deploy. Revert and redo it properly — take one side, then reapply the other change in the builder.

And add the merge driver, because the next person will make the same mistake.

58 How do you train a team on this?Medium

Write down the conflict-prone list, the ownership assignments and the resolution rule for each — flows unmergeable, labels union, value sets append-only.

People resolve conflicts carelessly when there is no stated rule, including on the conflicts that matter.

Config drift (59–76)

59 An admin fixed a validation rule in production on Friday.Hard

Your branch no longer matches production, and Monday's deployment silently reverts their fix.

Close the loop: nightly drift detection, retrieve into a config/* branch, PR into main, merge down to develop and open release branches the same day.

60 How does drift detection actually work?Medium

A scheduled job retrieves key metadata from production and diffs it against main, failing loudly on divergence.

It is the highest-value automation in a Salesforce pipeline, and its run history is the artefact an auditor wants.

61 Delta deployments hide drift. Is that a problem?Medium

Yes — full deployments enforce git-as-truth by overwriting; delta deployments leave untouched metadata alone, so divergence accumulates invisibly.

The mature answer is delta deployments plus scheduled drift detection.

62 Drift keeps recurring despite warnings.Medium

People bypass a slow process. The fix is a sanctioned fast path — a hotfix branch and a twenty-minute pipeline — not more enforcement.

Lock down production edit rights only once the corridor exists.

63 How do you capture a production change properly?Medium

Retrieve it into a config/* branch, review it in a PR like any other change, merge to main, then merge down everywhere.

Reviewing it matters: an urgent production change is exactly the kind that was made without consideration.

64 What should you review weekly?Easy

Setup Audit Trail — who changed configuration outside a release. Cheap, and it names the behaviour rather than the person.

Pair it with the drift-detection output so you have both the detection and the explanation.

65 Should humans be able to edit metadata in production?Medium

Ideally not — deployments happen as a service account, and humans go through the pipeline.

But only once the sanctioned fast path is genuinely fast, otherwise you have moved the problem somewhere you cannot see.

66 Drift detection is noisy with false positives.Medium

Usually metadata that churns harmlessly (generated layout XML, dashboard positions) or things you should be ignoring anyway.

Narrow the scope to metadata you genuinely control, and add the noisy types to .forceignore.

67 What drift does metadata detection miss entirely?Hard

Everything that is data: CPQ and Field Service configuration, Custom Metadata rows, custom setting values, queue members.

Those need their own versioned migration and a reconciliation check. Stating this unprompted is what makes an audit answer credible.

68 A sandbox refresh wiped your drift baseline.Medium

Drift detection should run against production versus main, not against a sandbox — sandboxes are expected to diverge.

If you also want sandbox drift detection, treat it as a different, lower-priority signal.

69 How do you baseline an org with no version control?Medium

Retrieve production into main, accept that it is ugly, tag it baseline, convert to source format and .forceignore the hostile metadata.

Trying to make the baseline beautiful is how this exercise burns three months.

70 What comes after the baseline?Medium

A read-only pipeline: PR validation that deploys nothing, plus nightly drift detection.

It builds trust and reveals the real change volume before you block anybody's work.

71 Hard-coded Ids broke after a sandbox refresh.Easy

Record Ids differ per org and per refresh. Resolve by name at runtime, with the name in Custom Metadata.

Scheduling policies, record types, queues and price books are the usual offenders. This habit prevents a whole category of post-deployment incidents.

72 How do you prove git matches production to an auditor?Hard

The drift-detection job and its run history, main equals production as an enforced invariant, tagged releases, deployment by a service account, no human metadata rights, weekly audit-trail review, and ticket ids in merge commits.

Plus the honest caveat about config that is data.

73 Who owns drift remediation?Medium

A named person with a cadence — unowned detection is no detection.

And the remediation must be same-day, because an uncaptured change compounds with every subsequent release.

74 Is drift always bad?Medium

No — an urgent fix made in production is sometimes the right call. What is bad is undetected, uncaptured drift.

The goal is not zero production changes; it is zero production changes that are not in git by the end of the day.

75 Drift events are trending down. What does that mean?Easy

The sanctioned path is working — people are using it rather than routing around it.

It is the most meaningful single metric for whether your process has actually been adopted.

76 A metadata type is not retrievable.Medium

Check the Metadata Coverage Report — some types are not supported, which means they cannot be drift-detected or deployed either.

Those become documented manual steps with their own review, rather than silent gaps.

Gates, flags & judgement (77–100)

77 What gates belong on each branch?Medium

Feature: lint, Jest, static analysis, fast unit tests. develop: delta validation against integration, full local tests, one reviewer. release/*: validation against production, migration dry run, release manager. main: everything plus drift check, two reviewers, no direct pushes.

The gate matches the blast radius of the target.

78 What do you look for in a Salesforce PR specifically?Medium

Bulkification, deliberate sharing keywords, FLS enforcement, assertions in tests, no hard-coded Ids — plus: did a profile sneak in, is the flow change deliberate, are destructive changes listed, and what here is data rather than metadata.

That last question is the Salesforce-specific one most reviews miss.

79 Branch protection on main — what settings?Easy

No direct pushes, required status checks, required reviews, up-to-date-with-main, and linear history.

Without enforcement, the invariant is a convention — and conventions decay under pressure.

80 Why do feature flags matter for branching?Medium

They let incomplete work merge switched off, which is what makes short-lived branches and continuous deployment possible.

They also make pulling a feature from a release trivial instead of a risky revert.

81 Which flag mechanism for which case?Medium

Custom Permission for per-user progressive rollout. Custom Metadata for environment-level flags that ship with the release. Hierarchy Custom Setting for runtime toggles editable in production.

The last is both the feature and the risk — it can be changed without a deployment.

82 You have forty live feature flags.Medium

Each is a branch in your code that must be tested in both states — so forty flags is harder to reason about than the long-lived branches you avoided.

Add a removal ticket in the same sprint you add a flag, and review live flags every release.

83 How do you migrate a legacy org onto a branch model?Hard

Baseline into main and tag it; convert to source format with hostile metadata ignored; read-only pipeline plus drift detection; automate deployment tier by tier with production last; then close production edit rights; then decompose into packages.

In that order. Reordering it is how the effort stalls.

84 A declarative-only team has no git experience.Medium

The branch is a record of what changed, retrieved by the pipeline rather than hand-written, and the PR exists for the review conversation.

Change sets as the deployment mechanism with git providing history is a legitimate intermediate state, not a failure.

85 How many long-lived branches is too many?Medium

More than you have orgs to validate against, or more than the team can keep forward-merged.

Each long-lived branch is a drift surface and a merge obligation. Two or three is typically the practical ceiling.

86 Someone force-pushed to a shared branch.Medium

Recoverable via the reflog, but it is a process failure: shared branches should be protected against it.

Add the protection, and make sure people know to rebase their own topic branches rather than shared ones.

87 Rebase or merge for keeping a feature branch current?Medium

Either, but be consistent — and with unmergeable metadata, rebasing repeatedly means resolving the same flow conflict repeatedly.

Merging develop in periodically is usually less painful for Salesforce metadata, at the cost of a messier history.

88 A branch has been open for three months.Hard

Assess honestly: is it still wanted? If yes, merge develop in now and then weekly, and break the remaining work into shippable pieces.

If the merge is already unmanageable, re-implementing on a fresh branch is sometimes genuinely faster.

89 What cannot a branching strategy fix?Hard

Too few sandboxes, no test coverage, a monolithic org serving unrelated business units, config living as data, production edits normalised by a slow process, and nobody owning the release.

Recognise more than two of those and the branch model is not your bottleneck. Fix the bottleneck first.

90 Two business units argue over releases. Split the org?Hard

Ask first whether they share customers, processes or reporting. If yes, splitting destroys the single customer view and you keep the argument.

Release conflict is usually governance and branching wearing an architecture costume: parallel release branches, clear metadata ownership, then unlocked packages.

91 How do you handle a vendor working in your org?Medium

Their own branch and sandbox, PRs into develop with the same gates, and no production access.

Undocumented vendor changes in a shared sandbox are a common source of drift and of mysterious regressions after they leave.

92 Your pipeline is good but nobody follows the branch model.Medium

Enforce what matters mechanically — branch protection, required checks, the forward-merge staleness check — and document the rest.

A model that relies entirely on discipline will erode; a model with three enforced invariants survives.

93 How do you measure whether the model works?Medium

Lead time from commit to production, change failure rate, mean time to restore, validation duration, and drift events per month.

Drift trending down and MTTR in tens of minutes are the two that tell you people are actually using the process.

94 An auditor asks who approved a production change.Medium

The PR into main, its reviewers, the linked ticket id, and the tag it shipped in.

That chain only exists if direct pushes are blocked and ticket ids are required — which is why those two controls are worth enforcing.

95 A hotfix bypassed review during an incident.Medium

Sometimes correct. Record it as an exception with the reason, and review it retrospectively.

What matters is that the bypass is visible and rare. An untracked bypass becomes the normal path.

96 How do you onboard someone to your branch model?Easy

A one-page document: the branches and what each means, the org each maps to, how to start work, how to release, how to hotfix, and the conflict-prone metadata rules.

If it does not fit on a page, the model is probably too complicated.

97 The team wants to change the model mid-programme.Medium

Establish what problem it solves and whether a smaller change fixes it — frequently the complaint is merge pain, which is branch lifetime.

Changing topology mid-flight costs real time, so it needs a clear benefit rather than a preference.

98 What is the most common branching anti-pattern?Hard

Routine cherry-picking to assemble releases — it means shipping untested combinations and a fictional history.

Closely followed by long-lived feature branches, profiles in version control, text-merged flows, and skipping the forward-merge after a hotfix.

99 Your first week on an org with no version control.Hard

Baseline production into main, accept the ugliness, tag it, convert to source format with hostile metadata ignored.

Then a read-only pipeline and nightly drift detection — which builds trust and shows you the real change volume before you block anyone's work.

100 One habit that most improves branching?Hard

Keeping branches short-lived, and enforcing the forward-merge mechanically.

Short branches eliminate most metadata conflicts before they exist; the automated staleness check eliminates the one discipline failure that silently reverts a production fix. Everything else is detail.
