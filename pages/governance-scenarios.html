Governance — 100 Scenarios

Centre of excellence and intake, technical debt, org strategy, release and change management, documentation and standards, licence and cost control, and the political reality of saying no.

📚
How to use this Answer out loud in ninety seconds, then open the answer. Concepts on Governance and its Advanced section.

Operating model (1–24)

1 What does a Centre of Excellence actually do?Medium

Owns standards, intake and prioritisation, the release process, architectural decisions and platform health — with enough authority to decline work.

A CoE that only advises becomes a documentation team nobody consults.

2 There is no governance at all. Where do you start?Hard

Intake first — a single route for requests with a named decision-maker. Without it, everything else is undermined by work arriving sideways.

Then standards, then release process. Trying to introduce all three at once fails.

3 Governance is seen as bureaucracy.Hard

Usually because it is — process with no visible benefit. Make it deliver faster decisions and fewer broken releases, and justify every step by what it prevents.

Governance that slows work without reducing risk deserves the reputation.

4 Who should be on the governance body?Medium

A business decision-maker who can prioritise, a technical owner, someone for data and security, and the delivery lead.

Small enough to decide, senior enough that the decisions hold.

5 Requests arrive from everywhere.Medium

One intake route with a required problem statement, requester, business value and urgency — and nothing starts outside it.

The discipline is refusing to begin work that arrived by a tap on the shoulder.

6 An executive bypasses intake.Hard

Accept the priority, insist on the process — log it, size it, and show what it displaces.

The trade-off conversation is the control; blocking an executive is not a viable one.

7 How do you prioritise a backlog?Medium

Business value against effort and risk, with explicit capacity for debt and platform health.

Without a reserved slice for maintenance, debt only ever gets worse.

8 How much capacity for technical debt?Hard

A standing allocation — often around a fifth — protected rather than negotiated each sprint.

Debt work that competes with features always loses, which is how orgs become unchangeable within a few years.

9 How do you justify debt work to the business?Hard

In their terms: deployment failure rate, time to deliver a change, incident count, hours lost to rework.

"The code is messy" is not fundable; "every change to Account takes three weeks because of this" is.

10 Admins are building in production.Medium

Decide deliberately what may be changed in production and what may not — some configuration reasonably is, code and critical automation are not.

A blanket ban nobody follows is worse than a clear boundary everyone does.

11 Who may install AppExchange packages?Medium

Nobody without review — security review, permission footprint, limit consumption, support model and exit path.

Packages are code you did not write with permissions you did not choose.

12 A business unit bought its own Salesforce org.Hard

Understand why — usually the central team was too slow — then decide whether to absorb it, federate it, or let it stand with integration.

Shadow IT is a symptom; punishing it does not address the cause.

13 Single org or multiple orgs?Hard

Single for shared customers, shared processes and a single view — with the cost of coordination and shared limits. Multiple for genuine separation: different regulators, independent businesses, acquisitions.

Consolidating later is one of the most expensive programmes in the platform.

14 Two orgs need to merge after an acquisition.Hard

Scope it properly: data model reconciliation, duplicate customers, automation conflicts, integration remapping, identity, licences, and the process differences nobody documented.

Underestimating this is routine; it is a programme, not a project.

15 How do you decide to build or buy?Medium

Buy where the need is common and the package is well supported; build where the process is your differentiator or no package fits.

And weigh the long-term cost of a package you cannot change against code you must maintain.

16 A vendor's package is causing problems.Hard

Document the impact with evidence, escalate through the vendor, and assess the exit cost — which you should have understood before installing.

Packages with no exit path are a strategic risk regardless of their current behaviour.

17 How do you manage limit consumption across teams?Hard

Monitor the org-wide consumables — API calls, storage, async queue, email — and attribute usage so teams can see their own footprint.

Shared limits are a tragedy of the commons unless consumption is visible.

18 Storage is near capacity.Medium

Archive deliberately with a retention policy, deduplicate, and review attachments and field history — not buy more storage reflexively.

Retention policy is the control; buying capacity defers the decision.

19 Nobody knows what an org contains.Hard

Inventory it: objects and their usage, automation per object, Apex and test health, integrations, installed packages, and unused fields.

You cannot govern or safely change what has never been enumerated.

20 Half the fields appear unused.Medium

Verify with field usage data before removing anything — then deprecate in stages: hide from layouts, mark deprecated, then delete.

Deleting a field someone's integration writes to is an outage.

21 How do you prevent duplicate functionality?Medium

Intake that checks whether it already exists, and an inventory that makes checking possible.

Three objects doing the same job is the standard consequence of intake without review.

22 Several teams build on the same org.Hard

Shared standards, a shared release calendar, ownership boundaries per object or domain, and a forum for cross-team changes.

The conflicts are mostly on shared objects — Account, Contact, Case — so those need named owners.

23 Two teams changed the same object in one release.Medium

Expected without coordination. A visible change calendar and object ownership prevents it.

Detecting it at deployment time is too late; the conflict belongs in planning.

24 What is the first sign governance is failing?Hard

Work arriving outside intake, and nobody able to say who owns a given object or automation.

Both appear long before the deployment failures and incidents that eventually force the conversation.

Change & release (25–50)

25 How often should you release?Medium

Frequently and in small batches — small releases fail less and are far easier to diagnose.

Monthly big-bang releases concentrate risk and make attribution of a failure nearly impossible.

26 Deployments keep failing.Hard

Look at the pattern: unreliable tests, environment drift, missing dependencies, or manual steps nobody documented.

Each has a different fix, and the pattern across failures tells you which one you have.

27 Production has drifted from source control.Hard

Measure the drift, reconcile deliberately, then close the route that caused it — usually unrestricted production changes.

Drift makes every subsequent deployment unpredictable, so it compounds quietly.

28 Is source control the source of truth?Medium

It must be, for anything deployable — otherwise you cannot know what is in production or reproduce an environment.

The gap between aspiration and reality here is the commonest DevOps problem on the platform.

29 How do you handle an emergency fix?Medium

A defined hotfix path: branch from what production has, minimal change, deploy, then merge back immediately.

The merge-back is the step that gets skipped, and skipping it means the next release reintroduces the bug.

30 A change caused a production incident.Hard

Restore service first — roll back or disable — then a blameless post-mortem producing a specific process or test change.

A post-mortem with no change to how you work has not finished.

31 How do you roll back a Salesforce deployment?Hard

There is no undo — rollback means deploying the previous version, and data changes may not be reversible.

Which is why the forward-fix path and feature flags matter more here than on platforms with real rollback.

32 Feature flags are proposed.Medium

Worth it — deploy disabled, enable for a pilot, disable instantly if wrong, without a deployment.

They are the closest thing to rollback the platform offers, and they need a cleanup discipline so flags do not accumulate.

33 How many sandboxes do you need?Medium

Developer sandboxes per stream, an integration environment, a UAT environment with representative data, and a full copy for performance and data work.

Fewer than that forces teams to share and interfere; more than that costs refresh effort.

34 Sandbox refreshes break everything.Medium

Script the post-refresh setup: identity configuration, named credentials, users, data seeding, and endpoints.

Post-refresh work done manually from memory is a recurring days-long cost.

35 UAT data is not representative.Medium

Then UAT tests nothing real. Seed representative, anonymised data as a repeatable process.

Testing against twelve clean records is how volume and edge-case defects reach production.

36 Should production data be copied to sandboxes?Hard

Only with anonymisation or masking for anything personal, and with access to full-copy sandboxes controlled as tightly as production.

A full sandbox of real customer data with loose access is a breach waiting to happen.

37 How do you manage the Salesforce release cycle?Medium

Read the release notes for your features, test in a preview sandbox, and plan for critical updates with deadlines.

Three releases a year is predictable work that should be in the calendar, not a surprise.

38 A critical update will break something.Hard

Test in preview, fix before the enforcement date, and track the deadline — they are enforced whether you are ready or not.

Unmanaged critical updates are the most predictable outage on the platform.

39 How do you decide what needs testing?Medium

By consequence: revenue paths, customer-facing flows, integrations and anything touching money. Automate those; test the rest proportionately.

Uniform testing effort across everything wastes it where it matters least.

40 Apex coverage is 76% and tests are meaningless.Hard

Coverage is a deployment gate, not a quality measure — tests with no assertions pass the gate and prove nothing.

Review assertions, not percentages, and treat coverage theatre as debt. See testing.

41 Flows have no tests.Medium

Common, and a real gap — flow tests exist, and critical flows should have them.

Automation migrating from Apex to Flow often loses its test coverage in the process, which nobody notices.

42 How do you review a change before release?Medium

Does it meet the requirement, follow standards, handle bulk and failure, enforce security, have meaningful tests, and avoid making future change harder.

The last question is the one reviews most often skip.

43 Code review is a rubber stamp.Medium

Make it specific with a short checklist, keep changes small enough to review properly, and automate what tools can check.

Large pull requests get approved without being read — the size is the problem.

44 Static analysis is suggested.Medium

Worth it in the pipeline for security patterns, SOQL in loops, missing sharing enforcement and the standard mistakes.

It catches the mechanical issues so humans can review design instead.

45 How do you enforce standards?Hard

Automate what can be automated, review what cannot, and keep the standards short enough to be read.

A fifty-page standards document is not followed; a one-page checklist in the pull request template is.

46 Contractors leave without documentation.Medium

Make documentation a deliverable with acceptance criteria, and conduct a handover before the final invoice.

Documentation requested after departure never arrives.

47 What documentation is actually worth keeping?Hard

Decisions and their reasoning, integration contracts, data model intent, operational runbooks, and anything non-obvious about why.

Not what the metadata already says — that documentation is always stale and always redundant.

48 Documentation is always out of date.Medium

Because there is too much of it. Document decisions and intent, generate what can be generated, and delete the rest.

Less documentation, kept current, beats more that nobody trusts.

49 How do you capture architectural decisions?Medium

Short records: the decision, the context, the options considered, the trade-off accepted, and the date.

It is the single highest-value document type, because it answers "why is it like this" two years later.

50 What most improves release reliability?Hard

Smaller, more frequent releases from source control, with meaningful automated tests on the critical paths.

Everything else is secondary to batch size — it reduces both failure rate and diagnosis time.

Cost, risk & compliance (51–76)

51 How do you control licence cost?Medium

Review utilisation — who has not logged in, who holds a feature licence they do not use — and right-size at renewal.

Licences accumulate because nobody reconciles them with actual usage.

52 Users are on more expensive licences than they need.Medium

Map what each population actually does to the cheapest licence that supports it, and plan the change at renewal.

The analysis needs usage evidence, not assumptions about what people need.

53 Renewal is in three months.Medium

Start the utilisation review now, with usage data per licence type and a clear position on what you need.

Renewals negotiated without usage evidence are negotiated from a weak position.

54 Consumption-based features are a new cost.Medium

AI credits, Data Cloud and platform events consume rather than seat-license — so they need monitoring and a budget owner.

Consumption costs surprise organisations used to predictable seat counts.

55 How do you monitor platform health?Medium

Limit consumption trends, deployment success rate, incident count, async queue depth, error rates and slow transactions.

Trends matter more than snapshots — the direction tells you what is about to become a problem.

56 API limits are being approached.Hard

Identify the consumers, move chatty integrations to bulk or composite patterns, and cache where appropriate.

Buying more limit capacity before fixing the chatty integration just raises the ceiling on the same problem.

57 An integration is consuming most of the API calls.Medium

Review its pattern — usually polling where events would serve, or per-record calls where bulk would.

And attribute consumption per integration so the conversation can be had with its owner.

58 Who is accountable when the org slows down?Hard

A named platform owner — otherwise shared limits and shared performance are nobody's problem.

Performance degradation is almost always cumulative, so it needs an owner watching trends rather than incidents.

59 How do you handle a security audit?Hard

Produce artefacts: permission holder lists with justification, sharing model, MFA enforcement and bypass paths, integration authentication, encryption decisions and review evidence.

"We will look into it" is the answer that turns an audit into a programme.

60 Who holds Modify All Data?Hard

You should be able to answer immediately with a justification per holder — and the answer should be very few.

Not knowing is itself the finding. See security.

61 How do you manage data retention?Hard

A policy per object stating how long, where it goes, and who approves deletion — then implement it with archiving rather than ad hoc deletes.

Retention is both a compliance requirement and the most effective storage control.

62 A customer requests deletion of their data.Hard

A documented process covering records, activity, files, backups, sandboxes and connected systems — with evidence it was completed.

Sandboxes and downstream systems are the parts most commonly missed.

63 Where is personal data in the org?Hard

You need a data map: which objects and fields hold personal data, its lawful basis, retention and who can see it.

Custom fields holding personal data nobody classified is the usual gap.

64 Is backup Salesforce's responsibility?Hard

No — under shared responsibility, protecting against your own bad deletes and corruptions is yours. Backup and Recovery or a third-party tool covers it.

And a backup you have never restored is not a backup.

65 When was the restore last tested?Hard

If the answer is never, that is the risk — and restore of relationships and hierarchies is substantially harder than export.

Test it on a real scenario and record the recovery time so the business knows what it actually is.

66 A mass delete went wrong.Hard

Check the Recycle Bin first, then backup. And review how a mass delete happened without a dry run — usually unrestricted data loader access in production.

Restrict destructive tooling and require a dry run on a subset.

67 Who may run data loads in production?Medium

A small named group, with a dry run on a subset, an extract taken first, and a documented rollback.

Production data tooling in many hands is one of the highest-probability data-loss risks.

68 How do you govern integrations?Hard

A register: what connects, direction, data, authentication method, owner, volume and error handling — reviewed periodically.

Undocumented integrations are discovered when they break during a change nobody knew would affect them.

69 An undocumented integration broke after a change.Medium

Restore, then add it to the register with an owner — and ask what else is connected that nobody knows about.

The register is built incrementally from exactly these events.

70 How do you assess a new requirement's risk?Medium

What it touches, who it affects, whether it is reversible, what it consumes, and what happens if it fails.

Irreversibility is the dimension most often ignored and most expensive to get wrong.

71 A requirement needs an irreversible change.Hard

Slow down — master-detail conversions, field type changes, org strategy and data model decisions deserve more scrutiny than reversible work.

Spend the design time in proportion to the cost of being wrong.

72 How do you handle an org health check finding?Medium

Triage by actual risk rather than fixing everything — some findings are genuine exposures, others are defaults that do not apply to you.

Document the accepted risks with reasoning so the next review does not re-litigate them.

73 What risks should be formally accepted?Medium

Any you have decided not to fix — written down, with the reasoning and the accepting person.

Unrecorded accepted risk is indistinguishable from an oversight when something goes wrong.

74 How do you report platform status upward?Medium

A small set of trends: delivery throughput, deployment success, incidents, limit headroom, licence utilisation and the debt position.

Six numbers with direction beats a long report nobody reads.

75 The business thinks the platform is slow to change.Hard

Measure it — cycle time from request to production, and where the time actually goes.

It is usually intake and decision latency rather than build time, and that is a governance problem you can fix.

76 What is the commonest governance failure?Hard

No intake, so work arrives from everywhere and priorities are set by whoever asked most recently.

Followed by no reserved capacity for debt, and no named owners for shared objects.

Judgement & influence (77–100)

77 How do you say no well?Hard

Not "no" — "yes, and here is what it displaces", or "here is a cheaper way to get the outcome you want".

Governance that only refuses gets routed around; governance that offers alternatives gets used.

78 A stakeholder insists on a bad design.Hard

State the concern once, clearly, with the specific consequence and cost. If they still want it, document the decision and build it well.

It is their call; your job is to make sure it is an informed one.

79 You were overruled and it went wrong.Hard

Fix it without saying "I told you so" — and use the decision record in the post-mortem to improve how such decisions are made.

The record is for process improvement, not vindication.

80 Everything is urgent.Medium

Make capacity visible and ask stakeholders to rank against each other rather than individually asserting urgency.

A list in order is a conversation; a list of priority-one items is not.

81 Governance is new and resisted.Hard

Introduce one thing that visibly helps — usually intake, because it ends the chaos of competing requests.

Earn credibility with a win before adding process.

82 How do you build credibility as a new platform owner?Medium

Fix something visible and painful quickly, inventory the org, and be straight about what you find.

Announcing a governance framework in week one, before anyone trusts you, fails.

83 An inherited org is in poor condition.Hard

Inventory, stabilise the worst risks, establish intake and release discipline, then remediate incrementally with reserved capacity.

A big-bang rebuild is rarely fundable and rarely necessary.

84 Should you rebuild or remediate?Hard

Remediate, almost always — rebuilds lose undocumented business logic and take longer than estimated.

Rebuild only a bounded, well-understood subsystem, and never the whole org.

85 Technical debt is genuinely unmanageable.Hard

Quantify its business cost — delivery time, failure rate, incidents — and propose a funded remediation programme with milestones.

Debt only gets funded when it is expressed as money and time.

86 How do you keep standards current?Medium

Review them periodically against what teams actually do, and change the standard when the practice is better than the rule.

Standards nobody follows should be fixed or deleted.

87 A team ignores the standards.Medium

Find out why — usually the standard is impractical, unexplained, or unenforced everywhere.

Selective enforcement destroys standards faster than no standards at all.

88 How do you onboard a new developer?Medium

Org inventory, the standards checklist, the deployment path, the decision records, and a small real change in week one.

Time to first deployment is a good measure of how healthy your platform practice is.

89 Knowledge is concentrated in one person.Hard

A genuine business risk. Write down the decisions, pair on changes, and rotate ownership.

The fix is documentation of intent plus shared experience — neither alone is enough.

90 How do you measure governance effectiveness?Hard

Cycle time from request to production, deployment success rate, incidents caused by change, debt trend, and whether work arrives through intake.

If governance is working, delivery is faster and more predictable — not slower.

91 Governance slowed delivery down.Hard

Then it is wrong. Find the step adding time without reducing risk and remove it.

Each process step should be justifiable by a specific failure it prevents.

92 How much process is right for a small team?Medium

Much less — source control, a release path, decision records and a prioritised backlog may be sufficient.

Enterprise governance on a two-person team is pure overhead.

93 How do you scale governance as the team grows?Medium

Add what the new scale requires: ownership boundaries, a change calendar, cross-team review — triggered by actual pain rather than anticipated.

Process added before it is needed is the process that gets resented.

94 A consultancy proposes a governance framework.Medium

Judge it on what it changes about decisions and releases, not on its diagrams.

Frameworks that produce documents rather than different behaviour are expensive.

95 How do you handle competing business units?Hard

A shared prioritisation forum with transparent capacity, so trade-offs are made between them rather than by you.

Arbitrating privately makes you the bottleneck and the villain.

96 The platform roadmap conflicts with a project.Medium

Surface it early with the specific conflict and options — building something a platform feature will supersede in two releases is worth knowing.

Reading the roadmap is part of the job.

97 How do you decide what not to build?Hard

When the value is small, the maintenance is lasting, a standard feature nearly does it, or it makes future change harder.

The work you decline is as much of a contribution as the work you deliver.

98 What is the highest-value governance artefact?Hard

The decision record — it prevents re-litigating settled questions and explains the org to whoever inherits it.

Cheap to produce and the thing most missed in its absence.

99 One hour to assess an org's governance. What do you ask?Hard

How does work arrive; is source control the source of truth; who owns Account; who holds Modify All Data; when was restore last tested; what capacity goes to debt.

Those six answers describe the maturity accurately.

100 One habit that most improves governance?Hard

Writing down decisions as they are made — the context, the options, the trade-off accepted and the date.

It compounds: it prevents repeated arguments, explains the org to newcomers, and makes post-mortems about process rather than blame.
