Governor Limits — 100 Scenarios

Which limit you actually hit, whether async helps, the org-wide ones that fail silently, how to measure before you breach, and how to design work that degrades gracefully.

📚
How to use this Answer out loud in ninety seconds, then open the answer. Concepts on Governor Limits and its Advanced section.

Diagnosing which limit (1–16)

1 "Too many SOQL queries: 101". Immediate cause?Easy

A query inside a loop. The limit counts queries, not rows — one query returning 10,000 rows is fine.

Collect Ids into a set, query once outside the loop, build a map. If the query is inside a called method, the method needs to accept a collection instead.

2 "Too many query rows: 50001".Easy

Total rows retrieved across all queries in the transaction. Note it does not increase in async context — a common misconception.

Fix with a selective filter, an explicit LIMIT, or Batch Apex with a QueryLocator, which streams outside this limit.

3 "Too many DML statements: 151".Easy

DML inside a loop. Statements, not rows — one update of 10,000 records is one statement.

Accumulate and perform one DML per object after the loop. Savepoints also consume statements, which surprises people.

4 "Apex CPU time limit exceeded" but queries are efficient.Hard

CPU counts your computation, not waiting. Query time, callout time and commit time are all excluded.

So the cost is loops, collection work, JSON, string building, or formula evaluation during your DML. Read CUMULATIVE_LIMIT_USAGE, which also attributes CPU per namespace.

5 A transaction runs 40 seconds without breaching CPU.Medium

It was mostly waiting — database time and callouts. Wall clock and CPU are different measurements, which is why timing with a stopwatch misleads you.

Conversely a compute-heavy transaction can fail at 11 seconds of wall clock. Always read the cumulative limits section.

6 "Apex heap size too large".Easy

6MB synchronous, 12MB async — and 60 fields across several objects for a few thousand records reaches it quickly.

Query fewer fields, avoid parallel copies of the same data, null out large collections when done, and prefer a query locator over holding a list.

7 How do you tell whether the limit is yours or a package's?Medium

CUMULATIVE_LIMIT_USAGE and LIMIT_USAGE_FOR_NS in the debug log break consumption down by namespace.

That log naming their namespace is also the artefact that gets a vendor support case taken seriously.

8 A previously-fine trigger starts failing after a package install.Hard

The package's triggers run in your transaction and CPU is shared, even though some limits are namespace-scoped. Your budget shrank.

Slim your logic, push heavy work async, and take the per-namespace evidence to the vendor if their consumption is unreasonable.

9 Your code works at 1 record and fails at 200.Easy

Something is per-record that should be per-transaction — a query, a DML, a describe, or a callout.

The bulk test at 200 records is the standard shape that catches this before review.

10 A limit error appears only in production.Medium

Data volume and shape: more related records per parent, more automation installed, more packages, and queries that have crossed the selectivity threshold.

Reproduce with production-shaped data in a Full sandbox rather than tuning blind.

11 The error names a limit you have never heard of.Easy

Check whether it is per-transaction or org-wide — that single distinction determines whether code tuning can help at all.

Org-wide limits (API calls, async executions, email, storage, scheduled jobs) need usage reduction or monitoring, not refactoring.

12 You hit a limit inside a managed package you cannot see.Medium

You get the cumulative totals but not their internals. Isolate by disabling your own logic and re-measuring, so the evidence is clean.

Then reduce what you contribute to the shared transaction, and raise it with the vendor with the log.

13 "Too many callouts: 101" surprises you.Easy

A callout in a loop. Batch the payload if the remote API supports it, or move to Batch Apex with a small scope.

Also remember the cumulative 120-second callout time budget — three sequential 40-second timeouts exhaust the transaction before the third completes.

14 A flow fails with a limit error.Medium

Flows share the transaction's limits. Usual causes: an Apex action, subflow or callout inside a loop (the engine does not bulkify those), or a loop over a very large collection hitting the element-execution limit.

Move the collection work outside the loop, or move the whole operation into Batch Apex.

15 A limit error has no stack trace you can use.Medium

Uncatchable limit exceptions terminate the transaction abruptly. For async work, attach a Finalizer — it runs even then and can record the failure.

For synchronous work, you need limit snapshots logged before the breach, which is why instrumenting at 60% matters.

16 What is the first thing you read in the debug log?Easy

CUMULATIVE_LIMIT_USAGE — actual consumption of SOQL, DML, CPU and heap, per namespace.

Then CODE_UNIT_STARTED/FINISHED for the execution tree, which usually reveals automation you did not know existed.

Does async help? (17–30)

17 Which limits actually double in async?Medium

CPU (10s → 60s), heap (6MB → 12MB) and SOQL query count (100 → 200).

Not total query rows (50,000) or DML rows (10,000) — the two people most often assume increase. That is why "make it a batch" sometimes changes nothing.

18 You move a 200,000-row query into a Queueable. Does it work?Medium

No — row limits are unchanged. Only Batch Apex with a QueryLocator escapes them, because it streams rather than loading.

Queueable helps with computation over moderate data, not with volume.

19 CPU-bound work on 10,000 records.Medium

Async gives you 60 seconds, which may be enough — and Batch Apex gives you 60 seconds per chunk, which almost certainly is.

Also reduce per-record cost: cache describes, avoid nested loops, avoid string concatenation in loops.

20 Does moving to async reduce total platform load?Medium

No — it moves it and consumes your daily async allocation. The benefit is per-transaction headroom and getting work off the user's request.

If the total work is the problem, you need less work (incremental processing), not a different context.

21 Async work exhausted your daily allocation.Hard

Async executions per 24 hours are org-wide and licence-scaled. An uncapped retry loop consumes them quickly, and then all async work stops.

Cap retries, back off, dead-letter, and alert on the allocation rather than discovering it when everything halts.

22 Batch Apex still fails on DML rows.Medium

The 10,000 DML-row limit applies per chunk, so scope 2,000 creating five children each exceeds it.

Work backwards from DML rows to choose scope, then verify CPU and heap headroom and leave margin.

23 Why does finish() have tight limits?Easy

It is a separate execution with its own modest allocation, intended for notification and chaining — not for processing.

Put real work in execute(), and keep finish() to reporting and starting the next stage.

24 Chaining Queueables indefinitely — any limit?Medium

Depth is effectively unbounded in production (and tightly limited in tests), but every hop consumes async allocation.

So the practical limit is your daily allocation and the risk of an accidental infinite chain. Carry a hop counter and cap it.

25 How many batch jobs can run at once?Easy

A small number org-wide; the rest queue in the Flex Queue as Holding. You can reorder but not accelerate them.

A growing Holding count is your early warning that the nightly window no longer fits.

26 Platform events as a way around limits?Medium

They do give each subscriber a fresh transaction, which is genuinely useful — including for escaping mixed DML.

But publishing and delivery consume event allocation, and several subscribers multiply it. It is a different budget, not an absence of one.

27 Continuations and the long-running request limit.Hard

A Continuation parks the request server-side without holding a thread, so it does not consume a concurrent long-running request slot while waiting.

That is the correct tool for a long synchronous callout from a UI, and the alternative is degrading the whole org's interactive performance.

28 Does a trigger get its own limits per chunk of 200?Medium

No — limits are per transaction. Two DML statements of 200 records in one transaction share one budget, and your trigger runs twice within it.

A Data Loader job of 10,000 does arrive as separate transactions, which is why it behaves differently.

29 You need 60 seconds of CPU in a synchronous user action.Hard

You cannot have it — 10 seconds is the synchronous ceiling. The UX must change: start async work, return a job Id, and show progress.

Attempting to squeeze 60 seconds of computation into a user's save is a design problem, not a tuning problem.

30 What does async genuinely buy you?Medium

Per-transaction headroom (CPU, heap, queries), the ability to call out after DML, work off the user's request, and retry semantics.

It does not buy more rows, less total work, or any timing guarantee — and assuming otherwise is the most common async design error.

Org-wide limits that fail silently (31–46)

31 Users get errors whenever your integration runs.Hard

The concurrent long-running request limit — ten synchronous requests over five seconds per org, shared with interactive users. Your background work is consuming the slots.

The nastiest limit on the platform, because the symptom appears nowhere near the cause. Move the work async.

32 Integrations fail every afternoon.Medium

Daily API request limit exhaustion. Check /limits, then find the consumer — usually a polling job.

Replace polling with events or CDC, batch requests via Composite, and alert at a percentage of the limit.

33 Notification emails stopped and nothing errored.Medium

Daily single-email or mass-email limit, which is org-wide and fails quietly rather than throwing in your code path.

Batch into digests, or move to custom notifications and platform events which do not share that cap.

34 You cannot schedule another job.Easy

Org-wide scheduled Apex limit, usually reached by scheduling one job per region or entity.

Use one scheduled job that iterates, and audit CronTrigger for abandoned schedules.

35 Data loads start failing with a storage error.Medium

Data storage is org-wide and bounded by licence count plus purchased capacity — and nobody monitors it until loads fail.

Archive closed records, hard-delete what the recycle bin is holding, and put storage on your monthly platform health review.

36 Your event-driven integration stopped receiving events.Hard

Daily event delivery allocation, or the concurrent subscriber limit — zombie connections from crashed consumers consume slots.

Consolidate subscribers, keep payloads thin, and monitor allocation. Also check whether the subscriber has been down past the retention window, in which case events are permanently gone.

37 File storage filled up.Easy

Separate from data storage and frequently consumed by email attachments and old documents.

Audit large files, move archival content to external storage with links, and set retention expectations with the business before you run out.

38 A report export is throttled.Medium

Concurrent report and dashboard limits exist per org, and heavy exports also hit row limits.

Schedule large extracts off-peak, use the Bulk API or Analytics API for programmatic extraction, and reconsider whether a 400,000-row export is really the requirement.

39 How do you find your current org-wide usage?Easy

The Limits REST resource (/limits) or the Limits API — daily API calls, async executions, storage, events and more.

Query it on a schedule and alert at a threshold. These limits fail silently, so polling is the only way to know in advance.

40 A sandbox behaves differently from production on limits.Medium

Org-wide allocations scale with licences, and sandboxes have different (often smaller) allocations and different data volumes.

So a load that works in a Full sandbox can hit API limits in production, and vice versa. Test volume behaviour where the allocation matches as closely as possible.

41 Who should own org-wide limit monitoring?Medium

A named person, reviewing a dashboard monthly: storage, API, async, events, email, scheduled jobs — with alerts at a threshold.

Unowned monitoring is no monitoring. See platform health.

42 A managed package consumes your API calls.Medium

Some package integrations call out through your allocation. Identify consumers from API usage by user in Event Monitoring or the API usage page.

Then negotiate with the vendor or restrict the integration's frequency. You cannot tune what you have not attributed.

43 Bulk API versus REST for a large load — limit impact?Medium

Bulk API 2.0 consumes far fewer API calls per record and is designed for volume; per-record REST calls burn your daily allocation quickly.

For a migration, per-record REST is close to always the wrong choice.

44 You need a temporary increase for a migration.Medium

Some limits can be raised temporarily via Salesforce Support with lead time — but plan on the assumption they cannot.

Design the migration to fit: chunked, resumable, spread across a window. A plan that depends on an unconfirmed limit increase is not a plan.

45 Why do org-wide limits matter more than per-transaction ones?Hard

Per-transaction limits fail loudly at the point of the bug, with a stack trace, affecting one operation. Org-wide limits fail silently, affecting everything, with no stack trace and no obvious cause.

Which is why monitoring matters more than tuning for this category.

46 Your org is at 85% of several limits. What do you do?Medium

Treat it as a capacity plan, not an incident: identify the top consumers, reduce the cheapest-to-fix, and forecast growth against the remaining headroom.

Then set alerts lower than 85% so the next conversation happens earlier, with options.

Measuring & instrumenting (47–62)

47 How do you warn before breaching, not after?Medium

Capture Limits.getCpuTime(), query and DML counts and heap at key points, compare against Limits.getLimitX(), and publish a platform event at about 60%.

That turns a future incident into a ticket, and the event-based log is visible in production where debug logs are not.

48 Where do you put limit snapshots?Easy

At stage boundaries in expensive operations — after the queries, after the computation, after the DML — so the deltas tell you which stage is costly.

A single total tells you that it is expensive; the deltas tell you where.

49 You want to assert limit usage in a test.Medium

Assert SOQL or DML counts after stopTest for hot paths — it catches a query creeping into a loop, which is a genuine regression guard.

Keep it to the few paths that matter; asserting exact counts everywhere makes every refactor a test rewrite.

50 The debug log is truncated before the part you need.Medium

Lower the log levels: set everything to NONE except the one category you need (APEX_PROFILING=FINEST for limits, DATABASE=FINEST for queries).

Everything at FINEST produces a truncated log that answers nothing — and slows the transaction enough to change the behaviour you are investigating.

51 How do you profile a slow save?Medium

CODE_UNIT_STARTED/FINISHED gives you the full execution tree with timings — every trigger, flow, validation rule and package.

It frequently shows your code is a small slice, and that the cost belongs to automation nobody documented.

52 A query returns 3 rows in 4 seconds.Medium

Selectivity, not volume — the optimiser scanned to find those three rows.

Run the Query Plan tool: cost above roughly 1 means a table scan. Lead with an indexed selective filter, or request a custom index.

53 How do you measure CPU attributable to a package?Medium

LIMIT_USAGE_FOR_NS per namespace in the debug log, and by disabling your own logic to establish a clean baseline.

Two measurements — with and without your code — make the attribution defensible.

54 You need production evidence without debug logs.Hard

Instrument in advance: structured logging published as platform events, with the correlation id (Request.getCurrent().getRequestId()), the quiddity, and limit snapshots.

Platform events survive rollback; a log row written with DML does not — which is the most common logging mistake.

55 How do you track whether limit usage is getting worse?Medium

Log peak usage per operation to a record and report on the trend, alongside job durations and record volumes.

"We are at 40% of CPU on this path and it was 25% last quarter" is actionable; a single breach is not.

56 A limit is breached only for one user.Medium

Their data shape or their permissions differ — more related records, a larger territory, sharing that returns far more rows, or a record-type-specific path.

Reproduce as that user with their data. "Works for me" is almost always a data-shape difference.

57 Should you log every transaction's limit usage?Medium

No — the logging itself costs, and the volume is unusable. Log when crossing a threshold, plus sampling for trend data.

Threshold-based logging gives you signal; comprehensive logging gives you noise and a storage problem.

58 How do you find the most expensive transaction in your org?Hard

Event Monitoring's Apex execution events include CPU time and run duration — aggregate them to find the worst offenders.

Without Event Monitoring you are reduced to sampling debug logs, which means you will find what you happened to catch rather than what is worst.

59 Your instrumentation itself is causing limit problems.Medium

Logging with DML in a loop, or serialising large objects for every log line.

Publish events instead of DML, keep payloads small, log at thresholds rather than everywhere, and never serialise a whole collection into a log message.

60 How do you convince a team to instrument before optimising?Medium

Show one case where the suspected cause was wrong — which is most cases. On this platform the CPU-versus-waiting distinction alone changes the fix entirely.

An hour of instrumentation routinely saves days of speculative refactoring.

61 A limit error happens once a week at random.Hard

Look for a volume or concurrency trigger: a weekly integration, a particular record with unusually many children, a batch overlapping with interactive usage.

Correlate occurrences by user, hour and record. Intermittent is usually "a specific condition we have not characterised" rather than random.

62 What should every production error log capture?Medium

Class, method, message, stack, record Id, correlation id, quiddity, running user and the limit snapshot at failure.

That set answers most incident questions without a debug log, which is exactly when you need it.

Designing within limits (63–82)

63 How do you make a long job resumable?Medium

Persist a high-water mark ("processed up to this Id/date") and start from it. Then a failure resumes rather than restarting.

Combine with mid-loop limit checks that re-enqueue the remainder, so the job completes in passes instead of failing at 95%.

64 What does "graceful degradation" mean here?Medium

The feature reduces rather than the save failing: if an enrichment callout fails, save the record and queue the enrichment.

Users forgive missing decoration; they do not forgive losing their work. That trade should be an explicit design decision.

65 A reviewer asks you to remove a LIMIT.Medium

Push back. An unbounded query works until the data grows, then fails in production with no code change to blame.

If every record genuinely must be processed, that is Batch Apex, not a larger list in one transaction.

66 How do you choose a batch scope?Medium

Work backwards from the tightest limit per chunk — usually DML rows — then verify CPU and heap headroom and leave margin for data growth.

A chunk running at 90% of CPU today will fail when the records get slightly heavier.

67 Incremental or full processing?Medium

Incremental (filter on SystemModstamp since a persisted watermark) with a periodic full reconciliation.

That shape keeps the window viable as data grows, and the reconciliation catches whatever the incremental path missed.

68 You need to process related records for 10,000 parents.Hard

Batch over the parents with a scope small enough that the children fit in the row and DML limits — and query children per chunk, not per record.

Chunking by parent also groups locks, reducing contention.

69 How do you avoid lock contention at volume?Hard

Sort DML by parent Id so concurrent transactions lock in the same order, chunk by parent, reduce batch size, and prefer serial to parallel where contention is high.

And check the data shape — persistent contention usually means ownership or lookup skew.

70 Your design needs 300 queries.Medium

Restructure: one query per object with an IN clause and maps for lookups, rather than per-record or per-branch queries.

If 300 is genuinely irreducible, the operation needs splitting across transactions — which is a design change, not a tuning exercise.

71 How do you keep heap under control with large payloads?Medium

Parse once and discard, process in slices, avoid holding both the raw string and the parsed object, and never keep parallel collections.

For genuinely large payloads, transform in middleware and send Salesforce a smaller shape.

72 Should you cache to save queries?Medium

Within a transaction, yes — a static map avoids re-querying reference data. Across transactions, Platform Cache for expensive re-derivable data.

Every cache read must handle a miss, and never cache anything you cannot recompute or anything user-specific in the org cache.

73 How do you design for 10× data growth?Hard

Selective, indexed queries; incremental processing; bounded collections; resumable jobs; and archiving so the working set stays bounded.

Then measure usage trends so you see the wall before you hit it. Most limit incidents are predictable six months ahead.

74 Middleware or Apex for heavy transformation?Medium

Middleware, usually — better memory, retry and observability, and it does not consume your org's limits.

Keep Apex for logic that genuinely needs Salesforce context. "We did it in Apex because that is where the data is" is how orgs hit limit walls at scale.

75 A requirement cannot be met within limits.Hard

Say so early, with the specific limit and the number, and offer alternatives: async with a progress indicator, nightly batch, middleware, or a reduced scope.

Limits are a design constraint like a memory budget. The failure is discovering it in UAT, not having the constraint.

76 How do you stop automation cascades consuming limits?Hard

Guard entry conditions tightly, never react to fields the platform or the engine writes, converge on one automation per object per timing, and build the filtered working set first.

A cascade across async hops is invisible in a single debug log, so add a durable counter you can alert on.

77 Does more code mean more limit risk?Easy

Not directly — shape matters more than size. One query in a loop is worse than a thousand lines of straight-line logic.

What does scale with size is the number of places a query or DML can hide, which is the argument for selectors and single DML points.

78 How do you bound a recursive data structure traversal?Hard

Query level by level with a visited set and a depth cap, rather than recursing per record — which produces a query per node.

Hierarchies of unknown depth are a classic limit trap; the depth cap also protects you from a data cycle somebody created.

79 You need an audit trail of every change at volume.Medium

Writing a log record per change doubles your DML and storage. Consider Field Audit Trail, CDC streamed to an external store, or sampling if the requirement allows.

Push back on "log everything forever" — it is itself a limits and cost decision, and usually unexamined.

80 Two teams each build within limits and together they breach.Hard

Limits are per transaction and shared by everything on the save. Each team's code is fine in isolation and the sum is not.

That is a governance problem: one owner per object's automation, a budget per save, and a review when anything is added. See governance.

81 Is it ever right to design close to a limit?Medium

Only with headroom and monitoring. At 90% of CPU you are one data-growth increment or one package install from failing.

Target well under, and treat consumption trends as a leading indicator.

82 How do you document limit-related design decisions?Medium

In the code, next to the decision: why the scope is 50, why the query has this limit, what the measured consumption was and when.

Future maintainers otherwise "optimise" the scope upward and reintroduce the failure you solved.

Judgement (83–100)

83 A stakeholder says limits are "Salesforce being awkward".Easy

Reframe: they are the mechanism preventing one tenant consuming shared compute. That is why they are per-transaction and non-negotiable.

Then move the conversation to design: hitting a limit means the shape needs to change, not that the platform needs persuading.

84 Someone proposes buying a limit increase.Medium

Per-transaction limits are not purchasable. Some org-wide allocations can be increased commercially or temporarily via Support.

So distinguish the two before promising anything, and design on the assumption the per-transaction ones are fixed.

85 You are asked to estimate whether a design will fit.Hard

Do the arithmetic: records per transaction × queries and DML per record, and the row counts. Most limit problems are visible on paper.

Then spike the uncertain part — usually CPU per record — with a realistic volume in a sandbox.

86 A vendor says their package "has no limit impact".Medium

Verify it. Install in a sandbox and measure CPU, queries and DML on a representative save, with and without their logic.

CPU is shared regardless of namespace scoping, so "no impact" is rarely literally true.

87 Which limit do you worry about most in a new design?Medium

CPU for synchronous user-facing work, row counts for data processing, and the concurrent long-running request limit for integrations.

Those three cause the most incidents — the first visibly, the second predictably, the third mysteriously.

88 You inherit code that fails on limits weekly.Medium

Instrument first so you know which limit and which path, then fix the worst. Rewriting before measuring usually solves the wrong thing.

Add graceful degradation and resumability as you go, so the next failure is a ticket rather than an incident.

89 A fix would take two days; a workaround takes an hour.Medium

Take the workaround if it is safe and reversible, and schedule the fix with the reason recorded — a smaller batch scope buys time legitimately.

What you must avoid is the undocumented workaround that becomes permanent and surprises someone in a year.

90 How do you prevent limit problems rather than fix them?Medium

Bulk tests at 200 as standard, static analysis blocking SOQL and DML in loops, bounded queries as a review item, and consumption trends reviewed monthly.

Prevention is mostly process, not cleverness.

91 An integration partner wants to call you 50 times a second.Hard

That is roughly four million calls a day and will exhaust any allocation. Negotiate a different pattern: batch payloads, Bulk API, or events in the other direction.

Also rate-limit at the gateway and give each consumer its own integration user so you can attribute and cap them individually.

92 Users say the system is slow but no limits are breached.Medium

Limits and performance are different problems. Start with the Lightning Usage App for EPT, then component instrumentation and the network tab.

Frequently the cause is too many components each fetching their own data, which no limit error will ever tell you.

93 How do you explain a limit breach to a business owner?Easy

Plainly: a shared-platform cap was reached, here is what triggered it, here is the fix and the timescale, here is the interim mitigation.

Avoid blaming the platform — it invites "why did we choose this platform" rather than a conversation about the fix.

94 Should you build a limits dashboard?Medium

Yes, for the org-wide ones — storage, API, async, events, email, scheduled jobs — with alerts at a threshold and a named owner.

Those are the limits that fail silently, and a dashboard nobody owns is decoration.

95 A limit forces a worse user experience.Medium

Make the constraint visible in the design rather than hiding it: a progress indicator, a pending state, a "results as of" timestamp.

Users tolerate honest asynchrony. They do not tolerate an action that appears to work and silently did not.

96 You suspect a limit will be hit in six months.Medium

Raise it now with the trend data, and propose the work while it is cheap. "We are at 45% and growing 5% monthly" is a conversation; "everything stopped" is an incident.

Capacity planning is the most under-used governance activity on this platform.

97 An architect proposes a design that only just fits.Medium

Ask what happens at 2× volume, after a package install, and with the automation another team will add next quarter.

"It fits today" is not a design property. Headroom is.

98 What is the most misunderstood limit?Hard

CPU time — because people assume it is wall clock, and it excludes exactly the slow parts (queries and callouts).

Closely followed by the belief that async doubles row limits, and by the concurrent long-running request limit, whose symptom appears nowhere near its cause.

99 How do limits change how you design compared to other platforms?Hard

You design in terms of batches and budgets from the start: bulk-safe by default, bounded collections, work split across transactions, and asynchronous paths for anything heavy.

Elsewhere you can often add capacity. Here the capacity is fixed, so the shape has to be right.

100 Four questions that solve most limit problems?Hard

Is it per-transaction or org-wide? (If org-wide, code tuning will not help.) Is it CPU or waiting? (Read the cumulative limits, do not guess from the clock.) Does async actually fix this one? (CPU, heap and query count yes; rows no.) Is the collection bounded? (An unbounded query works until the day it does not.)

Those four, asked in order, resolve the overwhelming majority of limit investigations.
