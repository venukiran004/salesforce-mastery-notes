Field Service — Advanced Concepts

End to end through the hard half: how the engine really grades a slot, shifts and capacity, optimisation and bundling, complex work, the inventory and maintenance chains, the offline mobile architecture, the FSL Apex API — and everything that bites you in production.

📚
Read the basics first This page assumes the object model and the rules-versus-objectives distinction from Field Service. Start there if "Work Order vs Service Appointment" isn't yet automatic — everything here is the layer above it.
🔄
Names move, mechanics don't Field Service ships a managed package on top of core platform objects, and the package's setting names, Apex signatures and feature gates change between versions. The mechanics on this page — availability resolution, candidate grading, optimisation behaviour, offline sync — have been stable for years. Confirm exact field names, method signatures and limits against the Field Service Managed Package Developer Guide for your installed version before you build.

How the product is actually assembled

Field Service is not one thing. It is four things stacked, and a surprising amount of confusing behaviour comes from not knowing which layer you are looking at.

The four layers — and who owns what
Layer ② is a package you install and version. Layer ③ runs outside your transaction. Layer ④ has its own data lifecycle. None of them behave like ordinary Apex.
LayerWhat it gives youWhat it costs you
Core objectsStandard objects, standard sharing, normal triggers and flows, Metadata API deployment of fields and layoutsNothing unusual
Managed package (FSL)Dispatcher Console, the policy/rule/objective model, background recipes, the Apex scheduling APIIts own triggers run alongside yours; configuration is stored as records; upgrades need regression testing
Optimisation engineBulk re-sequencing, travel minimisation, bundlingAsynchronous and off-platform — you cannot call it and read the result in the same transaction
Mobile appTrue offline working, on-device flows, service reports, barcode and signature captureA second data model to design, prime and debug

Licences and permission sets — who can do what

Access is a combination of a licence (or permission set licence) and the package's permission sets. Getting this wrong is one of the most common reasons a go-live slips.

PersonaTypically needsNotes
DispatcherDispatcher permissions + Dispatcher Console accessNeeds broad visibility across territories — this drives your whole sharing design
Technician (employee)Technician/Resource permissions + a mobile licenceMust be an active Service Resource linked to the User and a territory member
Scheduling & optimisationThe optimisation entitlement plus a nominated running userThat user's record access limits what optimisation can see and move
ContractorA contractor licence, or Experience Cloud access with Sharing SetsUsually modelled as capacity-based resources — see Resources
Agent / CSRService Cloud plus appointment-booking componentsBooks via candidates or arrival-window slots, never the Gantt
🧑‍🔧
The optimisation running user is a real design decision Optimisation executes as a nominated user. If that user cannot see a territory's appointments or resources, the engine silently optimises a smaller world than you think — and the output looks merely "disappointing" rather than broken. Give it deliberately broad, documented access, and treat it as a licensed, monitored account.

Availability — the resolution stack

Every scheduling question reduces to "is this resource available in this window?" That answer is derived from five independent sources on every single calculation. Debugging scheduling means walking this stack in order.

What makes a resource available at 14:00 on Thursday
Availability is not a field. It is computed every time, from all five inputs, per resource per day.
InputWhat it definesWhere it bites
Operating Hours + Time SlotsThe recurring working pattern and which slot types applyOperating hours carry a time zone. A resource in another zone is available at different absolute times than the Gantt suggests
ShiftsExplicit dated working periods, usually generated from shift patterns or templatesWhere shifts exist they generally take precedence over the hours pattern. Mixing both models in one population is a reliable source of confusion — pick one
Service Territory MemberMembership of a territory, with effective dates and a relationship typeExpired membership is the number-one cause of "this engineer vanished from the Gantt"
Resource AbsenceBlocked periods, optionally all-day, typedOften created by HR integrations — which means a day's plan can evaporate without anyone in Salesforce doing anything
CapacityFor capacity-based resources: hours or work items per periodConsumed by scheduled work, so cancellations must release it or contractors silently go "full"

Territory design

Territories are the unit of scheduling, of optimisation runs, of the Gantt and usually of sharing. Their shape sets your performance ceiling.

✅ Territories that work

  • Sized so one dispatcher can hold a day in their head — tens of resources, not hundreds
  • A hierarchy: country → region → operating area, with resources in the leaves
  • Drawn around travel reality — drive time, rivers, motorways — not sales geography
  • Secondary membership used deliberately for edge-of-patch cover
  • Relocation membership for genuine temporary moves, with dates

❌ Territories that hurt

  • One national territory "so anyone can do anything" — the Gantt and optimisation both suffer
  • Heavily overlapping patches, so engineers cross each other all day
  • Territories mirroring the role hierarchy for sharing convenience
  • Membership maintained by hand with no effective-date discipline
  • Hundreds of micro-territories, so nothing can ever cover anything else
🕐
Time zones: the bug you will definitely have Operating hours, the territory, the resource's user record, the customer's account and the dispatcher's browser can all sit in different time zones. Times are stored in UTC and rendered in the viewer's zone. When someone reports "it booked 08:00 but the customer was told 09:00", write down all five zones before you touch a rule.

Skills, levels and expiry

Skill Requirements on the work (usually inherited from the Work Type) are matched against Service Resource Skills. Three details do most of the damage:

  • Skill level is numeric and the test is "at least" — a job requiring level 5 excludes every level-4 person outright. Levels invented without a written rubric become arbitrary exclusions nobody can explain six months later.
  • Skills have effective start and end dates — a certification that lapsed last night removes that resource from today's candidate lists. Feed expiry from your training system and alert before it happens.
  • Requirements are ANDed, and can sit at several levels — work order, line item or appointment. Three stacked requirements on one appointment is very often the reason nobody qualifies.

Resource preferences

Resource Preference records (on an Account or Work Order) express Preferred, Excluded or Required resources. Their power depends entirely on which rule or objective you enable:

PreferenceEnforced byEffect
RequiredA work rule (hard)Only those resources are candidates — powerful and dangerous. One holiday and the job becomes unschedulable
ExcludedA work rule (hard)Removes resources outright. The right tool for "never send this engineer to that customer again"
PreferredA service objective (soft)Ranks them higher but still allows anyone — almost always what the business actually means

Inside the scheduling engine

"Get Candidates" is not a search, it is a pipeline. Knowing the order tells you exactly where to look when the answer is wrong.

From an unscheduled appointment to a booked slot
Rules decide who is allowed; objectives decide which allowed slot wins. Nobody appears → it's the rules. The wrong person appears → it's the objectives.
💯
What the grade actually means A slot's grade is a weighted sum of that policy's objectives, normalised to 0–100. It is not a probability, not a quality score, and not comparable across policies — 62% under an emergency policy and 62% under a routine policy mean different things. Compare grades only within one policy, and treat a cluster of near-identical grades as a sign your objectives aren't discriminating at all.

The appointment time fields, precisely

A large share of "the engine ignored my window" tickets are really a misunderstanding of which field means what.

FieldMeaningSet by
Earliest Start PermittedDo not start before this — the hard left edgeYou, the Work Type, or automation
Due DateMust be finished by this — the hard right edge, where the SLA livesYou or entitlement automation
Arrival Window Start / EndThe promise made to the customer ("between 9 and 12")Booking process / Work Type
Scheduled Start / EndThe engine's plan — what the Gantt drawsScheduling and optimisation
Estimated DurationHow long the work takes, in the duration type's unitsWork Type, ideally refined from history
Actual Start / EndWhat really happenedThe technician, from the mobile app

The schedulable space is Earliest Start Permitted → Due Date, narrowed further by the arrival window if you enforce it. Squeeze those together and you will get "no candidates" no matter how many engineers are sitting idle.

📌
Pinning quietly kills optimisation A pinned appointment tells optimisation never to move it. Dispatchers discover the button, pin everything they care about, and within a month the engine has almost nothing left to rearrange — while everyone complains that optimisation "stopped working". Report on pinned counts weekly and train dispatchers that pinning is for genuine customer commitments only.

Work rules, in full

Every rule is a record of a given type, attached to one or more policies. These types cover almost every real implementation; exact names vary a little by package version.

Rule typeWhat it enforcesPractical notes
Match SkillsResource holds every required skill, at or above the required level, unexpiredThe most common cause of empty candidate lists
Match TerritoryResource is a member of the appointment's territory on that dateConfigure whether secondary and relocation memberships count
Working TerritoriesWhich territory the resource is working that dayNeeded when people move between patches day to day
Resource AvailabilityHours/shifts, absences and existing bookings — the real free gapsAlso governs whether overtime and extended slots are permitted
Appointment Visiting HoursThe customer's allowed hours, not the engineer'sOmit it and you will cheerfully book a supermarket at 03:00
Match Fields / Extended MatchA field on the appointment or work order must match a field on the resourceThe escape hatch: van type, security clearance, language, brand accreditation. Cheaper and clearer than inventing fake skills
Count RuleMaximum number of matching appointments per resource per period"No more than two boiler installs per engineer per day"
Required / Excluded ResourcesTreats Resource Preferences as hard constraintsSee the warning under Resources
Resource CapacityKeeps capacity-based resources inside their allowanceMandatory if you model contractors by capacity
MultidayAllows work longer than a single day to be scheduled across daysRequired for multiday appointments — see Complex work
Time / dependency rulesHonours appointment dependencies — start after finish, same resource, same startDependencies exist as data but are only enforced when the rule is in the policy
🚧
Every hard rule is a promise that work can still be scheduled Rules compose with AND. Six individually reasonable rules can intersect to zero candidates on a wet Tuesday in February, and nobody will know which one did it. Add rules one at a time, keep a bare diagnostic policy containing only availability and territory, and compare against it the moment scheduling fails.

Objectives and how to tune them

ObjectiveOptimises forSide effect when over-weighted
Minimise TravelLeast drive time or distance between jobsCustomers wait longer; the nearest engineer gets everything
ASAPThe earliest possible slotTravel explodes and the day fragments
Appointment PriorityHigh-priority work placed firstRoutine work is deferred forever and ages into complaints
Preferred ResourceThe account's preferred engineerKey people become bottlenecks and burn out
Minimise OvertimeStaying inside contracted hoursLate-day emergencies go unfilled
Skill LevelMatching skill to difficulty — often the lowest qualified personEither experts do trivial jobs, or juniors get sent to hard ones
Resource PriorityPreferring employees over contractors, or the reverseContractor capacity you are already paying for sits idle

A tuning method that actually works

  • Write the business rule in one sentence first — "protect the SLA, then cut travel, and only use contractors when employees are full". If you cannot write it, you cannot weight it.
  • Start with two objectives, not seven. Priority and travel get you most of the way.
  • Change one weight at a time and re-run the same day's data. Multi-variable tuning is unfalsifiable.
  • Measure, don't feel — total travel minutes, SLA hit rate, jobs per engineer per day, overtime hours, unscheduled count. Capture the baseline before you touch anything.
  • One policy per intent — routine, emergency, contractor fill, weekend — rather than one policy of compromise weights.
  • Re-tune seasonally. Winter demand, holiday cover and new territories all invalidate last year's weights.

Policy strategy

A Scheduling Policy is a named bundle of rules and weighted objectives. Which policy applies depends on how the appointment is being scheduled — and that catches people out:

Entry pointPolicy used
Dispatcher clicks Get Candidates or ScheduleThe policy selected in the console (defaults configurable)
An agent books an arrival windowWhatever policy the booking component or Apex passes in
Background recipe / auto-scheduleThe policy named on that recipe
An optimisation runThe policy on the optimisation request — frequently a different one
Your ApexWhatever policy Id you pass — hard-coding it is a classic post-refresh bug
🪪
Never hard-code a policy Id Policies are records, so their Ids differ in every org and in every refreshed sandbox. Resolve the policy by name at runtime, and keep the name in Custom Metadata rather than in code. This single habit prevents a whole category of post-deployment incidents.
Routine→ Emergency→ Contractor fill→ Optimisation→ Diagnostic (rules stripped)

Those five are a good starting library for almost any implementation — including the diagnostic one, which exists purely so you can bisect failures in minutes instead of days.

Appointment booking and arrival windows

Dispatcher scheduling and customer-facing booking are different problems. Booking has to return windows a human can choose from, quickly, without committing capacity prematurely.

ConceptWhat it does
Arrival windowThe customer-facing promise, derived from operating hours designated for booking — typically 2- or 4-hour bands
Graded slotsCandidate windows returned with grades, so the UI can rank "best" options or hide poor ones
Exact appointmentsBooking a precise start time instead of a band — more precise, far more brittle in the real world
Slot suppressionHiding low-grade or overtime slots from customers so they cannot pick something you'll regret
Hold versus bookReturning slots reserves nothing. Two agents can offer the same slot seconds apart — handle the collision explicitly
⏱️
Booking is synchronous and users are impatient Slot calculation cost grows with candidate resources × horizon length × number of rules. A booking screen that takes twelve seconds gets abandoned, and agents go back to the phone. Offer the next ten working days rather than ninety, keep the booking policy leaner than the dispatch policy, and cache nothing you cannot invalidate.

Optimisation, end to end

Scheduling places one appointment well. Optimisation rearranges many to make the whole day cheaper. Different engines, different failure modes.

Run typeScopeUse it for
Global optimisationA territory (or set of them) across a date horizon, in the backgroundThe nightly re-plan — the main event
In-day optimisationToday's remaining, undispatched workRecovering from a morning that went wrong
Resource schedule optimisationOne resource's dayTidying a single messy route
ReshuffleMoves other appointments aside to make room for one urgent jobEmergencies, from the console
Fill-in scheduleFills a specific gap for one resourceA cancellation left a hole at 11:00
Group nearby appointmentsFinds unscheduled work close to an existing visitGetting extra value from a trip already being made

Each run is tracked as an optimisation request record with a status and result. That record — not the Gantt — is where you diagnose a run that "did nothing".

Enhanced Scheduling and Optimisation

Modern orgs run the enhanced engine, a Salesforce-hosted service that replaced the older in-org optimiser. Practical consequences:

What you gain

  • Substantially faster runs over larger territories and longer horizons
  • A prerequisite for appointment bundling
  • Better travel modelling and multi-territory handling
  • Far less org CPU burned on optimisation

What changes

  • Some legacy behaviours and settings differ — re-test every policy after migrating
  • Feature availability is version- and licence-gated; verify before designing
  • It is still asynchronous — nothing about that improves
  • Custom Apex leaning on legacy internals may need rework
🗺️
Why travel is still high after optimisation In this order: ① objective weights — is travel actually beating ASAP and preferred resource? ② pinned and dispatched appointments the engine may not move. ③ territory shape and overlap. ④ arrival windows too narrow for any efficient route to exist. ⑤ wrong home base or start/end location on resources, making the first and last legs fictional. ⑥ a horizon or resource pool too small to find a better plan. ⑦ travel settings — street routing versus straight line, and whether the estimates resemble reality at all.
🧪
Calibrate travel before you trust any number If the engine believes a cross-city journey takes twelve minutes, every plan it produces is fiction and engineers will quietly stop following the schedule — which is much worse than a visibly bad plan. Compare a week of engine-estimated travel against actual start times from the mobile app and adjust until they broadly agree. Do this before tuning objectives, or you are tuning against a broken model.

Appointment bundling

Bundling merges several short appointments at the same place into one bundle appointment, scheduled and dispatched as a single visit. Meter reads, inspections and multi-unit sites are the classic cases.

PieceRole
Bundle policyThe overall named strategy, switched on per territory or work type
Bundle configThe limits: maximum bundle duration, maximum members, how member durations aggregate
Restriction policyWhich appointments may bundle together — same account, same location, same work type, same day
Sort policyThe order members are worked inside the bundle
Propagation policyWhich changes flow from the bundle down to its members, including status
Bundle membersThe original appointments, now children of the bundle

Bundle when

  • Many short jobs cluster at one address or site
  • Travel dominates the cost of the work
  • The jobs genuinely can be done in one visit by one person
  • You want one arrival promise, not fifteen
vs

Don't bundle when

  • Members need different skills or different resources
  • Each job carries its own SLA or its own customer promise
  • Reporting and billing must stay strictly per job and you haven't tested it
  • You haven't decided what happens when one member fails
🎛️
The part everyone forgets: unbundling Bundles are formed by a background job and can be unbundled automatically or by hand. Design that path deliberately: what happens to member statuses, actual times, products consumed and service reports when a bundle is broken after the technician has completed three of five members? Test it before go-live, because it will happen in week one.

Complex work, dependencies and multiday

Real jobs are rarely one person for two hours. Field Service expresses the awkward shapes through dependencies between Service Appointments on the same Work Order.

DependencyMeaningTypical use
Start After FinishB may start any time after A finishesSurvey, then install; concrete pour, then cure, then commission
Immediately FollowB starts as soon as A ends, no gapA hand-over that cannot wait — scaffolding up, work, scaffolding down
Same StartA and B begin togetherTwo different specialists needed simultaneously
Same ResourceWhoever does A must do BReturn visits where context matters — and the customer expects the same face
🔗
Dependencies are data; enforcement is policy Creating the dependency records does nothing on its own. The dependency/time rule must be in the scheduling policy and in the optimisation policy, or the engine will happily schedule the install before the survey and optimisation will merrily undo any manual fix. Check both policies — they are frequently different records.

Crew or dependency?

Use a Crew

  • The same people always work together
  • You want one schedulable unit on the Gantt
  • One arrival, one set of actual times, one report
  • Membership changes occasionally, not per job
vs

Use dependencies

  • The pairing differs job to job
  • You need different skills simultaneously
  • Each person's time must be tracked separately
  • Phases happen on different days

Crews are dramatically easier to schedule and optimise. Reach for dependencies only when the flexibility is genuinely required — every dependency is another constraint the engine must satisfy, and a chain of them can become unschedulable in ways that are painful to diagnose.

Multiday appointments

Work longer than one working day — a three-day installation — is handled as a multiday appointment rather than several appointments, provided the multiday rule is enabled. What to know:

  • The engine spreads the duration across consecutive working days, respecting hours, shifts and absences on each one.
  • Optimisation treats them as heavy, awkward blocks. They are frequently the reason a territory's plan looks poor — a single five-day job pins down a resource for the whole horizon.
  • Progress tracking is coarser — one appointment spanning days means one set of actual times, so if the business wants day-by-day sign-off you need separate dependent appointments instead.
  • Decide the interruption policy up front: can a multiday job be paused for an emergency, and who is allowed to do that?

Background automation: recipes, auto-dispatch and drip feed

A mature implementation schedules and dispatches most work without a human touching it. That machinery is configured in Field Service Settings rather than in Flow, and it runs asynchronously.

MechanismWhat it doesDesign notes
Scheduling recipesEvent-driven automations: auto-schedule newly created appointments, reshuffle for emergencies, fill a gap when something is cancelled, group nearby work, bundleEach names its own policy and conditions. Enable one at a time and measure — several recipes reacting to each other produces churn nobody can explain
Auto-dispatchA background job that dispatches scheduled appointments per territory, within a horizonChoose the horizon carefully: dispatch too early and the plan is frozen before the day settles; too late and technicians start blind
Drip feedReleases only the next n appointments to the technician's deviceExcellent for controlling the "cherry-picking" problem and for volatile days. Unpopular with technicians who like to plan their own day — a change-management decision, not a technical one
Optimisation scheduleNightly and/or in-day runs per territoryStagger territories so runs don't collide, and leave headroom before the working day starts
🌀
Automation loops are the classic production incident A trigger updates an appointment → a recipe reschedules it → the reschedule fires the trigger again. Because the engine is asynchronous, this loop is invisible in a single debug log and can run for hours before anyone notices. Guard every automation with an explicit condition ("only when status is None and no resource assigned"), never react to the fields the engine itself writes (scheduled start/end, assigned resource) unless you mean to, and add a circuit-breaker counter you can alert on.

Status transitions and status categories

Almost every implementation adds custom appointment statuses — "Travelling", "On Hold — Parts", "Awaiting Access". This is fine, and there is exactly one rule you must not break.

🏷️
Every custom status must map to a status category The engine, the Gantt, the mobile app and the reporting all key off the status category (None, Scheduled, Dispatched, In Progress, Completed, Cannot Complete, Cancelled) — not off your label. A custom status with the wrong category means appointments that optimisation won't move, work that never appears on a device, or jobs the engine still believes are open. When something behaves inexplicably around status, check the category first.
CategoryWhat the platform infers
NoneNot yet scheduled — fair game for auto-scheduling and optimisation
ScheduledPlanned but not released; optimisation may still move it
DispatchedReleased to the technician; generally protected from being moved
In ProgressWork has started — actual start captured; do not reschedule
CompletedFinished; frees capacity, triggers downstream billing and reporting
Cannot CompleteAttended but not finished — usually generates a follow-up appointment
CancelledWill not happen; capacity must be released and the gap ideally refilled

Use status transition settings to restrict which status can move to which, by profile or permission. This is how you stop a technician marking a job Completed without capturing parts, or a dispatcher reversing a completed visit. Combine with required fields and validation rules that are offline-safe — see Mobile.

Parts and inventory, end to end

The part of Field Service that most projects underestimate. A job that needs a part nobody has is not a scheduling problem, it is an inventory problem — and it is where the second visit comes from.

The physical chain: warehouse to van to job to return
Stock is always "quantity of a product at a location". A van is just a location that moves.
ObjectRepresents
LocationWarehouse, van, customer site or plant room. Flagged as an inventory location, optionally mobile
Product ItemQuantity of a product held at one location — this is "stock"
Product Item TransactionThe audit trail of every increase and decrease
Product RequiredParts a work order, line item or work type needs — the demand signal
Product Request / Line ItemA request to source parts, e.g. "send two pumps to van 12"
Product TransferMovement of stock between locations, with quantities sent and received
ShipmentGoods in transit, with tracking
Products ConsumedParts actually used on the job — decrements van stock and feeds billing
Return Order / Line ItemSending stock back: unused, faulty, or for warranty/repair
Serialised itemsTracked individually as Assets, not as quantities
🔧
Parts availability is not a work rule There is no out-of-the-box hard rule that says "only schedule this job to an engineer who is carrying the part". The practical patterns: ① don't release the appointment until parts are confirmed (a status/flag the scheduling recipe filters on); ② stamp a field on the appointment from your own parts check and use a Match Fields rule against the resource; ③ schedule anyway and accept a second visit, which is what most organisations actually do while claiming otherwise. Decide explicitly rather than by accident — first-time fix rate is usually the single biggest number the business cares about.

Assets, warranties and maintenance

Field Service's long-term value is in what you know about the equipment, not the visits. This is the layer that turns break-fix into planned work.

ConceptWhat it does
Asset hierarchyParent/child assets — a chiller inside a plant room inside a building. Faults and history roll up meaningfully
Asset RelationshipNon-hierarchical links between assets (replaced-by, connected-to)
Warranty Term / Asset WarrantyWhat is covered, for how long, and what percentage of parts, labour and travel the customer pays
Maintenance PlanThe recurring agreement that generates work orders ahead of time
Maintenance AssetWhich assets the plan covers — one plan can cover hundreds
Maintenance Work RuleThe generation pattern for a covered asset — calendar-based, or driven by usage criteria
Generation settingsHow far ahead work orders are created, how many at a time, and whether you get one work order per asset or one per plan

Three flavours of preventive maintenance

Calendar-based

Every six months, first Tuesday, annually on the anniversary. Simple, predictable, easy to sell.

Risk: visits happen whether or not the equipment needs them.

Usage / criteria-based

Every 500 operating hours, every 10,000 cycles — driven by readings held on the Asset.

Requires a reliable feed of those readings. No feed, no plan.

Condition-based

Telemetry or Data Cloud signals indicate degradation, and a work order is raised in response.

Highest value, hardest to do — needs real integration and a trusted threshold.

📅
Maintenance generation is a batch, and it fails quietly Work orders appear because a background job ran. When "the PPM visits didn't get created", check in this order: the plan's date range and next-suggested date, the generation horizon (it may simply not be time yet), whether the batch is scheduled and completing, whether covered assets are active and still linked, and whether the generated volume hit a limit. Monitor the job's outcome — do not wait for the business to notice a missed month.

Entitlements, SLAs, pricing and time

AreaHow it works in Field Service
Entitlements & contractsWork Orders can carry an Entitlement or Service Contract, so coverage is checked before an engineer is sent
Milestones & SLASLA start/exit tracking on the work order, with time-based milestone actions for warnings and escalations. Business Hours decide what "four hours" means
Due date as the contractIn practice the SLA becomes the appointment's Due Date — that is how scheduling learns to care about it
PricingWork Order Line Items price from a Price Book; totals (subtotal, discount, tax, grand total) roll up to the work order
BillingUsually an integration: completed work orders with consumed products and labour become invoices in ERP or Revenue Cloud
Time SheetsTime Sheet and Time Sheet Entry records capture work, travel and breaks per resource per period — the feed to payroll
⚖️
Two clocks, and they disagree The SLA clock runs on Business Hours from the entitlement. The scheduling clock runs on operating hours and shifts in the territory's time zone. If the contract promises four working hours but the territory's operating hours are different, appointments will be scheduled that breach the SLA while every rule passes. Reconcile the two deliberately, and translate the SLA into Earliest Start Permitted and Due Date on the appointment so the engine can actually honour it.

The mobile app: offline architecture in depth

The mobile app is not a thin client. It holds its own copy of a slice of your org, works fully offline, and reconciles later. Treat it as a distributed system, because it is one.

Priming, working offline, and reconciling
Nothing in the middle box can reach a server. Everything in the right-hand box happens later, in bulk, and possibly while the record has already changed in the org.
MechanismWhat to know
Offline primingConfiguration decides which objects, related lists and date range are downloaded. More priming = longer sync, more storage, staler data. Tighten the date window aggressively
Field-level uploadThe app generally uploads changed fields, which limits how often two people genuinely collide. It does not eliminate conflicts on the same field
Mobile flowsScreen flows that run on the device. Element support is restricted offline — no callouts, no server-side Apex, and Get Records only against primed data
Quick actionsThe main way to give technicians one-tap operations on the appointment
Lightning web componentsSupported in the app, with offline data access through the GraphQL wire adapter and device capabilities such as barcode scanning and location. Verify current targets and offline-supported adapters in the mobile developer guide
App extensionsLaunch another app, a URL, a flow or a component from the appointment — how you integrate a meter app or a manufacturer's diagnostic tool
Service reportsTemplate-driven completion documents with signature capture, generated on the device and synced
Push notificationsDrive awareness of new, changed or cancelled work; essential if you drip-feed
LocationResource geolocation feeds travel estimation, real-time tracking for customers, and "who is nearest" decisions. Also a works-council and privacy conversation — have it early
📴
Your validation rules fire at sync time, not at edit time A technician completes eleven jobs in a basement. On reconnect, a required field or a validation rule they could not satisfy offline rejects the upload — and now there is a sync error, in a queue, that they will not notice until tomorrow. Audit every validation rule, required field and trigger on the appointment and work order for offline-satisfiability: can a technician possibly meet this condition with only primed data? If not, move the check server-side into a status change or a scheduled job that raises a task instead of blocking the save.
♻️
The idempotency rule Post-sync automation must tolerate: the same record arriving twice, appointments completing out of order, actual end times before actual starts (clock skew and manual entry), and records referencing a work order someone else closed this afternoon. Write every post-sync handler so that running it twice changes nothing the second time, and never assume the current server state resembles what the device saw.

🎯
Where the money actually is Ranked by return in most field service programmes: ① avoid the visit (remote resolution, better triage) → ② fix it first time (right skills, right parts) → ③ cut travel (territories, bundling, optimisation) → ④ shave minutes on site. Teams almost always start at ④ because it feels technical, and wonder why the business case never lands.

Apex, automation and the FSL API

The managed package exposes an Apex API for candidates, booking, scheduling and optimisation. Three rules govern all of it: resolve policies by name, never call it synchronously from a trigger, and treat every call as expensive.

📖
Signatures vary by package version The samples below are illustrative of the shape of the API, not a contract. Method signatures, class names and return types have changed between package releases. Always confirm against the Field Service Managed Package Developer Guide for the version installed in your org before writing real code.

Offering and booking windows

/* Resolve config by NAME, return graded windows, then book one.
   Illustrative — verify signatures for your installed package version. */
public with sharing class FsBookingService {

    public class FsConfigException extends Exception {}

    // Policy Ids differ in every org and every refreshed sandbox.
    // Look them up by name; keep the name in Custom Metadata, not in code.
    private static Id policyIdByName(String policyName) {
        List<FSL__Scheduling_Policy__c> policies = [
            SELECT Id FROM FSL__Scheduling_Policy__c
            WHERE Name = :policyName
            LIMIT 1
        ];
        if (policies.isEmpty()) {
            throw new FsConfigException('Scheduling policy not found: ' + policyName);
        }
        return policies[0].Id;
    }

    // Arrival windows an agent or customer can choose from, with grades.
    public static List<FSL.AppointmentBookingSlot> offerWindows(
        Id appointmentId, String policyName, Id bookingHoursId
    ) {
        List<FSL.AppointmentBookingSlot> slots = FSL.AppointmentBookingService.getSlots(
            appointmentId,
            policyIdByName(policyName),
            bookingHoursId,
            UserInfo.getTimeZone(),
            false                      // false = arrival windows, true = exact times
        );

        // Never show a customer a slot you would not want them to pick.
        List<FSL.AppointmentBookingSlot> offered = new List<FSL.AppointmentBookingSlot>();
        for (FSL.AppointmentBookingSlot s : slots) {
            if (s.grade >= 40) offered.add(s);     // suppress poor-grade options
        }
        return offered;
    }
}

Scheduling and optimisation from an asynchronous context

/* Scheduling calls are heavy and hit the engine. Do them in Queueable,
   one appointment at a time, and record WHY a failure happened. */
public class FsAutoScheduleJob implements Queueable {

    private final List<Id> appointmentIds;
    private final String policyName;

    public FsAutoScheduleJob(List<Id> appointmentIds, String policyName) {
        this.appointmentIds = appointmentIds;
        this.policyName     = policyName;
    }

    public void execute(QueueableContext ctx) {
        Id policyId = FsBookingService.policyIdByName(policyName);
        List<ServiceAppointment> toFlag = new List<ServiceAppointment>();

        for (Id saId : appointmentIds) {
            try {
                FSL.ScheduleResult result = FSL.ScheduleService.schedule(policyId, saId);

                // A null/empty result is NOT an error — it means no candidate passed
                // every hard rule. Make that visible instead of silently losing the job.
                if (result == null) {
                    toFlag.add(new ServiceAppointment(
                        Id = saId,
                        Scheduling_Note__c = 'No candidates under policy ' + policyName
                    ));
                }
            } catch (Exception e) {
                toFlag.add(new ServiceAppointment(
                    Id = saId,
                    Scheduling_Note__c = 'Scheduling error: ' + e.getMessage()
                ));
            }
        }
        if (!toFlag.isEmpty()) update toFlag;
    }
}

/* Requesting an optimisation run — asynchronous by nature.
   You get an optimisation request record back; the plan changes later. */
public class FsOptimisationRequester {
    public static void optimiseTomorrow(List<Id> territoryIds, String policyName) {
        FSL.OAASRequest req = new FSL.OAASRequest();
        req.allTasksMode         = true;                        // include scheduled work, not just unscheduled
        req.start                = Date.today().addDays(1);
        req.finish               = Date.today().addDays(8);     // keep the horizon short
        req.locations            = territoryIds;                // one run per territory group
        req.schedulingPolicyID   = FsBookingService.policyIdByName(policyName);
        req.filterFieldAPIName   = 'FSL__Optimize__c';          // only appointments opted in

        new FSL.OAAS().optimize(req);
    }
}
⛔
Do not call the scheduling API from a trigger It is slow, it is effectively a round trip to the engine, and it cannot be bulkified per record. A trigger that schedules on insert will blow CPU limits the first time someone loads 200 work orders — and then quietly corrupt the plan when a retry re-runs it. Publish a platform event or enqueue a Queueable, and let a recipe or your own job do the work.

Post-sync automation that survives reality

/* Mobile data arrives late, out of order, and sometimes twice.
   Every post-sync handler must be safe to run again. */
public with sharing class AppointmentCompletionHandler {

    public static void onStatusChange(List<ServiceAppointment> appts, Map<Id, ServiceAppointment> oldMap) {

        Set<Id> toProcess = new Set<Id>();

        for (ServiceAppointment sa : appts) {
            ServiceAppointment prior = oldMap.get(sa.Id);

            // Key off the STATUS CATEGORY, not the label — custom statuses vary by org.
            Boolean nowComplete = sa.StatusCategory == 'Completed';
            Boolean wasComplete = prior != null && prior.StatusCategory == 'Completed';

            // The idempotency guard: a flag we set ourselves, once.
            if (nowComplete && !wasComplete && !sa.Completion_Processed__c) {
                toProcess.add(sa.Id);
            }
        }
        if (toProcess.isEmpty()) return;

        // Heavy work (billing hand-off, callouts, roll-ups) goes async and bulk-safe.
        System.enqueueJob(new CompletionProcessor(toProcess));
    }
}

AI in Field Service

The genuinely useful applications are narrow and unglamorous, which is exactly why they work.

Worth doing

  • Pre-work briefing — a summary of the asset's history and previous visits, so the engineer arrives informed
  • Post-work summaries from notes and transcripts, turning three words into a usable record
  • Knowledge surfacing for the specific fault code and asset model
  • Triage and deflection before a visit is ever booked
  • Duration prediction from history — better estimates improve every downstream plan

Handle with care

  • Anything writing to the schedule without a human check — the optimiser already owns that decision
  • Safety-critical procedure generation
  • Parts recommendations that create real cost with no confirmation
  • Summaries used as the legal record of what was done on site

See Agentforce A to Z for how agents, actions and guardrails are built, and Einstein, AI & Data Cloud for grounding and the Trust Layer. The field service specifics are simply that connectivity is unreliable and mistakes cost a truck roll.

End-to-end implementation blueprint

The order that keeps projects out of trouble. Each step depends on the one before it, and skipping ahead is how implementations end up re-doing territories in month six.

  • 1 · Define the operating model on paper — territories, shifts, skills, who may do what, and the three numbers the business will judge you on (first-time fix, SLA, travel).
  • 2 · Get the work taxonomy right — Work Types with honest durations and skill requirements. Every downstream plan inherits these numbers, so bad durations poison everything.
  • 3 · Build the availability foundation — operating hours, territories with dated membership, shifts or hours (pick one), absences fed from HR.
  • 4 · Load the resource picture — resources, skills with levels and expiry, crews, capacity for contractors.
  • 5 · Calibrate travel — before any tuning. Make the engine's estimates resemble reality.
  • 6 · Build the policy library — routine, emergency, contractor, optimisation and diagnostic. Two objectives each to start.
  • 7 · Prove it manually — dispatchers scheduling by hand in the console, with candidate lists they trust. If they don't trust the grades, nothing automated will be accepted.
  • 8 · Automate scheduling — one recipe at a time, measuring against the baseline after each.
  • 9 · Turn on optimisation — nightly, one territory as a pilot, comparing travel and SLA against the manual baseline before rolling wider.
  • 10 · Ship mobile carefully — tight priming, offline-safe validation, mobile flows for procedures, service report templates, and a real offline test with real devices.
  • 11 · Close the physical loop — van stock, product requests, consumption, returns. This is where first-time fix actually improves.
  • 12 · Add maintenance and the customer layer — maintenance plans to shift work from reactive to planned; reminders and tracking to cut no-access failures.
  • 13 · Instrument everything — a dashboard of unscheduled count, travel minutes, SLA hits, first-time fix, overtime, pinned appointments and sync errors. Review it weekly and tune from evidence.

Anti-patterns

Configuration

  • One giant territory, or hundreds of micro-territories
  • Ten hard rules stacked "to be safe", then nothing schedules
  • Custom statuses with no status category mapping
  • Skills invented for things that are really field matches
  • Required-resource preferences used where preferred was meant
  • Tuning objectives before calibrating travel

Build & operations

  • Calling the scheduling API synchronously from a trigger
  • Hard-coded policy Ids
  • Validation rules that cannot be satisfied offline
  • Post-sync automation that isn't idempotent
  • Priming three months of data to every device
  • Treating package config as deployable metadata
  • Pinning everything, then blaming optimisation
  • Building a custom Gantt instead of using the console

Interview questions

1 How does the engine decide which slot to offer?Medium

It's a pipeline. The candidate set starts as resources in the appointment's territory. Work rules filter it as hard constraints — fail one and the resource disappears. What survives becomes a gap matrix of every legal slot, built from availability (hours/shifts, absences, existing bookings) and travel. Service objectives then score each slot, weighted, producing a grade of 0–100, and slots are returned best-first.

The diagnostic consequence: if nobody appears it is always the rules; if the wrong person appears it is always the objectives.

2 A grade of 78% — what does that actually mean?Hard

It's a weighted sum of that policy's objectives, normalised to 0–100. It is not a probability, a confidence or a quality measure, and it is not comparable across policies — 78% under an emergency policy is a different statement from 78% under a routine one.

Two practical points: grades clustering tightly means your objectives aren't discriminating between slots, and you should never write business logic against an absolute grade threshold without documenting which policy it assumes.

3 Walk me through everything that determines whether a resource is available on Thursday afternoon.Hard

Five inputs, combined at calculation time: operating hours and time slots (with a time zone), shifts where used — which generally override the hours pattern, service territory membership and its effective dates, resource absences, and capacity for capacity-based resources. Then subtract existing appointments and the travel between them to get the real gaps.

Availability is never a field you can check — it's derived, per resource per day. That's why "he's definitely working Thursday" and the Gantt can disagree, and why expired territory membership is the most common cause of a resource apparently vanishing.

4 Optimisation runs nightly and changes almost nothing. Diagnose it.Hard

First the run itself: check the optimisation request record for status and result — it may be failing or finishing with nothing in scope. Then what it's allowed to touch: how many appointments are pinned, and how many are already dispatched or in progress? Dispatchers pinning everything is the single most common cause.

Then scope and configuration: is the request using the policy you think (optimisation policies are frequently different from console policies), is the horizon and territory set wide enough, and does the running user actually have access to those appointments and resources? A narrow running user optimises a smaller world with no error anywhere.

5 Crew or complex work dependencies?Medium

Crew when the same people always work together: one Service Resource of type Crew gets scheduled, one arrival, one set of actual times. Far easier to schedule and optimise.

Dependencies when the pairing varies, when you need genuinely different skills simultaneously, when each person's time must be tracked separately, or when phases fall on different days. Types include start-after-finish, immediately-follow, same-start and same-resource.

The trap: dependency records do nothing unless the dependency rule is in both the scheduling policy and the optimisation policy — otherwise the engine schedules the install before the survey, or optimisation undoes your fix overnight.

6 What changes in your design because the mobile app works offline?Hard

Three things. On-device logic can't reach a server — no callouts, no server-side Apex, no validation against unprimed data — so procedures go in mobile flows and simple field updates.

Validation fires at sync time, not edit time. Every validation rule, required field and trigger on the appointment and work order must be satisfiable offline, or a technician's whole day lands in a sync-error queue they won't notice until tomorrow. Checks that can't be satisfied offline move server-side and raise a task instead of blocking the save.

Post-sync automation must be idempotent and order-tolerant — the same record may arrive twice, completions may arrive out of sequence, and the work order may have been touched in the org since. Guard with your own processed flag, key off status category rather than label, and make re-running a no-op.

7 Why is "most of Field Service config is data, not metadata" such a big deal?Hard

Scheduling policies, work rules, objectives, territories, operating hours, shift patterns, skills and package settings are all records. Change sets and source deployments don't carry them, so promotion between environments needs a repeatable data migration kept in version control — not a person reconfiguring by hand at 2am.

The corollary that bites hardest: record Ids differ per org and per refreshed sandbox, so never hard-code a policy Id. Resolve by name at runtime, with the name in Custom Metadata. Almost every "it worked in UAT" Field Service incident traces back to one of these two facts.

8 Appointment bundling — when, and what breaks?Medium

Bundle when many short jobs cluster at one location and travel dominates the cost — meter reads, inspections, multi-unit sites. Configuration is a bundle policy plus a config (max duration, max members, duration aggregation), a restriction policy (what may bundle with what), sort and propagation policies. It requires the enhanced optimisation engine.

What breaks: members needing different skills or resources, per-job SLAs and customer promises, and per-job reporting and billing if you haven't tested it. The most commonly forgotten path is unbundling — decide now what happens to member statuses, actual times, consumed products and service reports when a bundle is broken after three of five members are done.

9 How do you make sure an engineer arrives with the right part?Hard

Start by being honest: there's no out-of-the-box hard rule for "carries the part". The chain is Product Required on the work → Product Request → Product Transfer/Shipment → Product Item at the van's Location → Products Consumed on completion → Return Order for what comes back.

The three workable patterns: ① don't release the appointment until parts are confirmed, with the scheduling recipe filtering on a readiness flag; ② stamp a field from your own parts check and enforce it with a Match Fields rule against the resource; ③ schedule anyway and accept the second visit. Most organisations do ③ while claiming ①. Choose deliberately — first-time fix is usually the number the business cares about most, and it lives or dies here.

10 Where would you put the logic to auto-schedule new appointments, and why not in a trigger?Hard

First choice is configuration: a scheduling recipe that auto-schedules appointments matching a condition, with its own policy. No code, supported, and the package owns the retry behaviour.

If it must be code, publish a platform event or enqueue a Queueable and call the scheduling API there. Not a trigger, because the call is slow, effectively a round trip to the engine, and can't be bulkified per record — a 200-record load blows CPU limits, and a retry re-runs it and corrupts the plan.

Whichever route, guard the condition tightly ("status category None, no assigned resource") and never react to fields the engine itself writes, or you'll build an automation loop that's invisible in a single debug log.

11 Salesforce Scheduler or Field Service?Easy

Scheduler is for booking appointments with people — branch meetings, advisers, consultations — where travel is irrelevant and there are no parts. Field Service is for sending people to places to do work, where travel is the dominant cost and you need assets, inventory, maintenance and an offline mobile app.

The test: if nobody drives anywhere and nothing gets fitted, Field Service is expensive machinery you won't use. If drive time is your cost driver, Scheduler will never get you there.

12 Travel times in the plan don't match reality. Why does it matter so much?Hard

Because every decision the engine makes is built on them. If it believes a cross-city trip takes twelve minutes, the plan is fiction, engineers quietly stop following it, and you lose the schedule as a source of truth — which is worse than a visibly bad plan, because nobody reports it.

Fix it before tuning anything: compare a week of estimated travel against actual start times from the mobile app, check street-level routing versus straight-line settings, and verify each resource's home base and start/end locations so the first and last legs aren't invented. Only then tune objective weights — otherwise you're tuning against a broken model.

13 A dispatcher says optimisation "used to work and now doesn't". Nothing was deployed. What happened?Hard

Almost always accumulated state rather than a change. Leading candidates: pinning has crept up until there's nothing left to move; dispatch is happening too early, so work is protected before optimisation runs; territory membership or shifts weren't generated for the new period; absence volume rose (seasonal holiday); appointment volume outgrew the horizon or the run window; or the optimisation job has been failing silently — check the request records.

Also worth checking: a package upgrade, an engine migration, or someone editing a shared policy record. Policies are data, so a "no deployment" change is entirely possible — and there's no deployment history to show you.

14 You're leading a Field Service implementation. What order do you do things in?Hard

Operating model on paper first — territories, shifts, skills, and the three numbers we'll be judged on. Then the work taxonomy (Work Types with honest durations, since everything downstream inherits them), then the availability foundation, then resources and skills.

Calibrate travel before tuning anything. Then build a small policy library — routine, emergency, contractor, optimisation, diagnostic — and prove it with dispatchers scheduling manually. If they don't trust the candidate grades, no automation will ever be accepted.

Then automate one recipe at a time against a measured baseline, pilot optimisation on one territory, ship mobile with tight priming and offline-safe validation, close the parts loop, and add maintenance and the customer layer. Instrument throughout — unscheduled count, travel minutes, SLA, first-time fix, overtime, pinned counts, sync errors — and tune from evidence rather than opinion.

Quick check

1. A custom appointment status behaves strangely — optimisation won't move those appointments. What do you check first?
The platform reasons about status categories, not your labels. A custom status mapped to Dispatched or In Progress is protected from being moved.
2. Where should code that calls the scheduling API live?
The call is slow and effectively a round trip to the engine. From a trigger it blows CPU limits on bulk loads and corrupts the plan on retry.
3. Candidate grades under one policy are all within a point of each other. What does that tell you?
A grade is a weighted sum of objectives. If every slot scores the same, the weights are too close or the objectives don't vary across these options — so the "best" slot is effectively arbitrary.
4. Promoting Field Service config from UAT to production — what's the main problem?
Most Field Service configuration is data. You need a repeatable, version-controlled data migration — and you must never hard-code the resulting Ids.
5. A technician's offline work vanished after a day with no signal. Most likely cause?
Validation fires at sync, not at edit. Anything unsatisfiable offline lands in a sync-error queue the technician won't notice — audit every rule for offline-satisfiability.
6. Dependencies exist between two appointments, but the engine schedules them in the wrong order. Why?
Dependency records are data. They're only enforced when the dependency rule is present — and it must be in both policies, or overnight optimisation will undo a correct plan.
