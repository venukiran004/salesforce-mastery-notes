Integration Patterns — 100 Scenarios

Choosing a pattern from requirements, consistency models and compensation, latency budgets, data ownership, middleware, throttling, observability and the architecture conversations that decide everything.

📚
How to use this Answer out loud in ninety seconds, then open the answer. Concepts on Patterns & Approach and its Advanced section.

Pattern selection (1–18)

1 A user clicks a button and needs a credit score before continuing.Easy

Request and reply — the caller waits because the answer determines what happens next.

Budget the latency explicitly, set a timeout, and decide what happens when the remote system is slow or down: block, or proceed with a default.

2 An order must be sent to the ERP, but the user should not wait.Easy

Fire and forget — publish a platform event or enqueue async work, and let the user's save complete.

Then you owe the design a retry path, a dead letter and a reconciliation, because nobody is watching the result.

3 Nightly customer master updates from the ERP.Easy

Batch data synchronisation — Bulk API with upsert on an External Id, scheduled.

Cheapest to build and operate, easiest to re-run, and perfectly adequate when the business genuinely only needs daily freshness.

4 Users need to see live stock levels from the warehouse.Medium

Data virtualisation — Salesforce Connect external objects, or a callout rendered in a component. Nothing is stored.

Accept that the warehouse's availability and latency become your page's. If that is unacceptable, cache a summary with a visible timestamp.

5 A button must trigger a process in another system and show the result.Medium

Remote call-in from the UI — an Apex callout invoked by the component, with the result rendered.

If the remote process is slow, use a Continuation so you do not consume a long-running request slot, and show a progress state.

6 An external system needs to create cases in Salesforce.Easy

Remote process invocation inbound — an Apex REST endpoint with a business contract, idempotency key and validation.

Not raw sObject access, which couples them to your schema and gives you nowhere to put logic or logging.

7 Two systems both let users edit the same customer record.Hard

Bidirectional sync, which doubles the complexity and demands conflict resolution. First challenge it: decide ownership per field, and most "bidirectional" requirements collapse into two one-way flows.

If genuinely bidirectional: stable cross-system keys, explicit conflict rules, loop prevention markers, and reconciliation.

8 How do you choose between patterns in an interview?Hard

Drive it in order: who owns the data, what latency the business actually needs (challenge "real-time"), volume and shape, the consistency requirement and what compensates for failure — then the pattern and the technology.

Naming MuleSoft or platform events in the first thirty seconds skips every decision that matters.

9 The business says "real-time". What do you ask?Medium

Decompose into a number: must the user see it before finishing the task (~2 seconds), within seconds but nobody waiting, within minutes, or is overnight fine?

Very often the real requirement is "I do not want to explain stale data", which a visible last-synced timestamp solves more cheaply than any architecture.

10 One pattern or several for one integration?Medium

Several is normal and healthy: events for freshness, batch for completeness, request-reply for the one interactive lookup.

Insisting on a single pattern for everything produces either excessive cost or inadequate guarantees.

11 You are asked to replace nightly batch with events.Medium

Ask what decision improves with fresher data. If none does, the batch is cheaper to run, easier to debug and simpler to recover.

If freshness genuinely matters, add events alongside the batch rather than replacing it — the batch becomes your reconciliation.

12 A partner can only send files.Medium

File-based batch: they drop a file, middleware or a scheduled job picks it up, validates and loads via Bulk API.

Build in a manifest or checksum so a truncated file is detected, and an error file they can act on — file integrations fail silently more than any other kind.

13 Should Salesforce be the orchestrator?Medium

Rarely for multi-system processes. Apex has tight limits, poor retry primitives compared with middleware, and no good way to model long-running sagas.

Let middleware orchestrate and let Salesforce own the customer-facing process and data.

14 How do you integrate with a system that has no API?Hard

Options in order of preference: a database view exposed through middleware, file exchange, an RPA or screen-scraping layer (last resort).

Be explicit about the fragility of the last option, and isolate it behind a facade so replacing it later does not touch Salesforce.

15 A requirement needs data from four systems on one page.Hard

Four synchronous callouts is four latencies and four availability dependencies. Prefer one aggregating call to middleware that fans out and composes.

Or pre-compute the aggregate asynchronously and render it with a freshness timestamp.

16 When is point-to-point acceptable?Medium

One producer, one consumer, a stable contract, and no expectation of more consumers. Then middleware is ceremony.

It stops being acceptable at the third consumer of the same data, or when the system of record is likely to be replaced.

17 How many integrations is too many point-to-point?Medium

The arithmetic answers it: n systems point-to-point tends toward n² connections to maintain, each with its own auth, error handling and monitoring.

Somewhere in the high single digits, a hub or API layer becomes cheaper than the mesh.

18 A stakeholder wants the "simplest possible" integration.Medium

Agree, and define simple as simplest to operate, not fastest to build. Nightly batch with reconciliation is usually simpler to run than a real-time stream.

The complexity you remove at build time often reappears at 3am.

Consistency & compensation (19–36)

19 Can you have a transaction across Salesforce and an ERP?Easy

No. There is no distributed transaction, so atomicity across systems is not available.

You choose an inconsistency to live with: eventual consistency with reconciliation, or compensating actions for failure.

20 Step three of a four-step process fails.Hard

You need a compensating action for steps one and two — the business-level reverse of each.

Design those at design time; asked in production it becomes a data-repair project. And each compensation must itself be idempotent, because it will be retried.

21 What does eventual consistency mean in practice?Medium

The systems converge after a bounded delay, and a reconciliation repairs anything that did not.

Make the delay explicit and visible to users — a last-synced timestamp turns "the data is wrong" into "the data is from two minutes ago".

22 A finance team insists both systems must always match.Hard

Explain what is achievable: they will match within a defined window, and a reconciliation proves it daily with an exception report.

That is usually what they actually need — evidence of correctness, not instantaneous equality, which no distributed system provides.

23 Where should the compensation logic live?Medium

With the orchestrator — typically middleware, which can hold saga state across systems and retry each step independently.

Modelling a saga in Apex is possible and awkward; the limits and the lack of durable workflow state work against you.

24 How do you detect divergence?Medium

Business-level reconciliation: counts and checksums per period compared between systems, with an exception report of the differences.

Comparing logs tells you what you sent; comparing counts tells you what arrived. Only the second catches silent loss.

25 Reconciliation found divergence. Who fixes it?Medium

Decide in advance: automatic repair where the source of truth is unambiguous, human review where it is a genuine conflict.

And name the owner. A reconciliation exception report with no owner accumulates for months and then nobody trusts it.

26 What is an idempotency key, concretely?Easy

A value the caller generates that uniquely identifies the intent — not the attempt. The receiver stores it and returns the same result for a repeat.

Enforce it with a unique database constraint, which is the only guarantee that holds under concurrency.

27 A timeout occurred mid-process.Hard

Unknown outcome, not failure. The remote step may have succeeded.

Query by correlation id to determine the true state before deciding to retry or compensate. Assuming failure is how duplicate payments happen.

28 Read-your-writes consistency — when does it matter?Medium

When the user who made the change would be confused not to see it — their own order, their own case update.

Achieve it by updating locally first and syncing outward, so the initiating user sees it immediately even though others see it later.

29 Last-write-wins is proposed. Is it acceptable?Medium

Sometimes, if the business accepts that a concurrent edit can be silently overwritten — and if "last" is determined by a reliable timestamp.

It is unacceptable for anything where both edits carry intent (two people adding different notes). Then you need field-level ownership or merge.

30 Clock differences break your last-write-wins rule.Hard

Do not trust client or remote clocks for ordering. Use a server-assigned version number or sequence per record.

Clock skew between systems is routine and produces conflict resolution that is wrong in a way nobody notices.

31 How do you prevent an update loop in two-way sync?Medium

A source marker: each system stamps which system originated the change, and ignores inbound changes it caused.

Alternatively compare values and skip no-op updates — but the marker is more reliable because a no-op can still differ in formatting.

32 Deletes are not handled in your sync design.Medium

The commonest gap. Decide whether a delete propagates, soft-deletes, or is ignored — and remember Salesforce deletes are recoverable from the recycle bin while the downstream hard delete may not be.

Soft delete plus a status field is usually the safest default for synced data.

33 A merge in Salesforce breaks downstream references.Hard

Merging deletes the losing records, so external systems holding their Ids now point at nothing.

Publish a merge event carrying both the surviving and the merged Ids so consumers can re-point. Very few designs handle this, and it surfaces as mysterious orphans.

34 Partial success on a 200-record inbound batch.Medium

Return per-record results so the caller knows exactly which failed and why, and process the rest.

All-or-nothing on a large batch means one bad row blocks 199 good ones, and the caller has no way to make progress.

35 How do you handle an out-of-order update?Medium

Carry a version or source timestamp in the payload and reject anything older than what you already hold.

That makes the operation safe regardless of arrival order, which is far more robust than trying to guarantee ordering.

36 The business asks what happens if an integration fails.Medium

Answer in their terms: what the user sees, what data is affected, how long until it self-heals, and what manual step exists if it does not.

If you cannot answer those four, the failure handling is not designed — it is hoped.

Latency, volume & throttling (37–54)

37 How do you set a latency budget?Medium

Work backwards from the user experience: total acceptable time, minus your own processing, leaves the remote call's budget — which becomes the timeout.

Then verify the remote system's realistic p99 fits inside it. If it does not, the pattern is wrong.

38 A remote system's p99 is 8 seconds.Hard

Too slow for a synchronous user path, and it will consume concurrent long-running request slots that interactive users share.

Make it asynchronous with a pending state, or use a Continuation if it must be user-initiated and synchronous.

39 Your integration degrades the UI for everyone.Hard

The concurrent long-running request limit — ten synchronous requests over five seconds per org, shared with real users.

This is the single most important reason to keep integration work asynchronous, and its symptom appears nowhere near its cause.

40 A partner floods your API.Medium

Rate-limit at the gateway, give each consumer its own integration user so you can attribute and cap them individually, and return 429 with Retry-After.

Inside the org, bound page sizes and reject oversized payloads rather than trying to process them.

41 You are the noisy neighbour on someone else's API.Medium

Respect their rate limits proactively: cap your concurrency, batch payloads, back off on 429 with jitter.

Getting throttled or blocked by a partner is slower to resolve than designing within their limits in the first place.

42 What is backpressure and do you have it?Hard

The consumer controlling how much it receives. Pub/Sub API provides it; CometD does not; a REST endpoint has none unless you implement rate limiting.

Without backpressure, a fast producer plus a slow consumer means a growing backlog and eventually lost data.

43 A brief outage turned into a long one.Hard

Retry storm: everything retried simultaneously the moment the system came back.

Exponential backoff with jitter, plus a circuit breaker that fails fast during the cooling period, prevents your recovery from causing a second outage.

44 How does a circuit breaker work here?Medium

After n consecutive failures, stop calling and fail fast for a cooling period, then allow a trial call before fully reopening.

Store the state where all transactions can see it (a custom setting or record), since statics do not span transactions.

45 Volume grew 10× and the integration broke.Medium

Usually per-record calls that were fine at low volume. Move to batch payloads or Bulk API, and process incrementally.

Also check your API allocation consumption — growth often hits the org-wide limit before it hits any code limit.

46 How do you plan for growth?Medium

Measure current volume and allocation consumption, project it, and identify which limit you reach first and when.

Most integration limit incidents are visible six months ahead if anyone is looking.

47 A nightly window no longer fits.Medium

Make the job incremental (process only what changed since a persisted watermark), reduce per-record cost, then stagger against other scheduled work.

Parallelising adds lock contention, so measure rather than assuming it helps.

48 Should you throttle your own outbound calls?Medium

Yes — cap concurrency deliberately (batch scope, number of parallel jobs) rather than discovering the remote system's limit by being rejected.

It also makes your load predictable, which is what makes capacity conversations with the other team possible.

49 Large payloads are causing failures.Medium

Page the remote API, request fewer fields, accept gzip, and bound inbound payloads with a clear rejection.

If a single logical payload is genuinely huge, the transformation belongs in middleware, not in Apex heap.

50 Caching remote data — when is it right?Medium

When the data is read far more than it changes, and you can define a freshness requirement and an invalidation strategy.

Without invalidation you have created a second system of record that silently diverges — which is worse than the latency you were avoiding.

51 Users complain data is stale.Easy

Show them how stale: a last-synced timestamp on the page. It converts an unbounded complaint into a factual statement they can evaluate.

Frequently that is sufficient, and it is dramatically cheaper than reducing the sync interval.

52 You need sub-second response from a slow system.Hard

You cannot make their system faster, so change the architecture: pre-compute and cache what the UI needs, with a defined refresh and a visible freshness indicator.

Or reduce what the UI needs synchronously to the one value that genuinely gates the user's next step.

53 How do you measure integration latency properly?Medium

Log duration per hop with the correlation id, then report p50, p95 and p99 — averages hide the tail that users actually notice.

Separate your processing from remote wait time, so you know whether to optimise your code or escalate to the other team.

54 A requirement needs both low latency and completeness.Hard

Use both patterns: events for immediacy and a periodic batch for completeness, with the batch acting as reconciliation.

Trying to get both guarantees from one mechanism is what produces fragile, over-engineered designs.

Data ownership & MDM (55–72)

55 Who owns the customer record?Hard

Decide per field, not per object — the ERP may own billing address and credit terms while Salesforce owns relationship data and preferences.

Write it down in a field-level ownership matrix. It is the single most useful artefact in any integration programme.

56 Both systems create customers independently.Hard

You need a matching strategy and cross-system keys, or you will accumulate duplicates on both sides.

Either nominate one system as the creator (the other requests creation), or implement real matching with a human review queue for ambiguity.

57 There is no stable key between systems.Hard

Then you are doing fuzzy matching, and fuzzy matching produces both duplicates and wrong merges.

Establish a key: have one system generate it and the other store it. That one-off exercise is cheaper than permanent matching ambiguity.

58 Where should the External Id live?Easy

On both sides: Salesforce holds the remote system's key in an External Id field, and ideally the remote system holds the Salesforce Id.

That makes upserts idempotent in both directions and makes reconciliation possible.

59 A canonical data model is proposed.Medium

Valuable with several systems exchanging the same concepts — it decouples each system's schema from every other.

It is overhead for two systems. And a canonical model nobody governs becomes a third schema to maintain.

60 Is Salesforce the right MDM hub?Hard

Sometimes for customer data where Salesforce has the richest view — but it is not an MDM tool: no survivorship engine, limited matching, and storage costs.

Data Cloud or a dedicated MDM platform is usually the better hub, with Salesforce as a consumer.

61 How do you handle reference data (countries, currencies)?Medium

One owner, distributed outward — ideally as Custom Metadata so it deploys with releases, or synced from a single source.

Reference data maintained independently in each system diverges within months and produces reporting nobody can reconcile.

62 A field means different things in each system.Medium

A semantic mismatch, which is worse than a format mismatch because the mapping looks correct.

Document the meaning, not just the mapping — "Status = Active means billing is live, not that the customer is engaged" — and transform explicitly.

63 Picklist values differ between systems.Easy

A mapping table, owned by one side and version-controlled — ideally Custom Metadata so it deploys and is testable.

And handle the unmapped value explicitly: log it and route for review rather than silently dropping or defaulting it.

64 An unmapped enum value arrives.Medium

Do not throw and do not silently default. Log it with the value, route the record to an exception queue, and alert.

External systems add values without telling you, so this path will be exercised.

65 How much transformation belongs in Salesforce?Medium

As little as possible. Middleware has better tooling, memory and observability for mapping and transformation, and it does not consume your limits.

Keep Salesforce logic to what needs Salesforce context.

66 A downstream system needs a field you are about to rename.Medium

If you exposed raw sObjects, the rename is a breaking change you cannot see the consumers of.

This is the argument for DTOs and business contracts: your schema becomes an implementation detail you can change freely.

67 How do you track who consumes what?Medium

A register: integration name, direction, objects and fields, consumer, owner, auth method, schedule and API version.

Without it you cannot assess the impact of any change, and every schema change becomes a gamble.

68 Two teams both claim to own the same data.Hard

This is the actual integration problem, and it is organisational. Escalate to a decision rather than engineering around it.

Building a bidirectional sync to avoid the conversation gives you a permanent technical cost and the conversation anyway.

69 How do you handle historical data at cutover?Medium

Decide explicitly how much comes across. Full history is usually unnecessary, expensive and slows the migration.

Keep the rest in a read-only archive or the legacy system. This decision saves more migration time than any technical optimisation.

70 Data quality in the source is poor.Medium

Integrating it faithfully propagates the problem. Decide what you reject, what you cleanse in transit, and what you accept with a quality flag.

And feed the exceptions back to the source owner — otherwise you become the permanent cleansing layer.

71 Should you integrate or consolidate?Hard

If two systems serve the same purpose for the same users, consolidation may be cheaper long-term than permanent integration.

Integration is often the default because it avoids a political decision — and then it is maintained for a decade.

72 How do you know an integration is no longer needed?Medium

Log usage and volume, and review the register annually against the business processes that justified each one.

Orgs carry integrations for systems that were decommissioned years ago, because nobody can prove they are unused.

Middleware & observability (73–88)

73 What does an API-led layering actually buy you?Medium

System APIs isolate each system of record, process APIs hold orchestration, experience APIs shape per consumer — so replacing a back-end system touches only its System API.

That is the whole payoff. For one point-to-point feed it is overhead.

74 Middleware is proposed. How do you evaluate it?Medium

Against the number of consumers, the transformation and orchestration needs, the retry and observability requirements, and whether a system of record is likely to change.

Also honestly: who will operate it, and do they have the skills? Unowned middleware becomes a black box.

75 Correlation ids — where do they come from?Easy

Generated at the edge (the first system to handle the request), passed in a header at every hop, stored on the record, logged everywhere.

Non-negotiable. Debugging a multi-system flow without one is guesswork, and adding it later means instrumenting everything again.

76 What should you log, and what should you not?Medium

Log direction, endpoint, status, duration, attempt, correlation id and record Id. Do not log sensitive payloads.

Full payload logging creates a second copy of your data with weaker controls, and it is a finding in any security review.

77 Which metric gives the earliest warning?Medium

The age of the oldest unprocessed item. A rising number tells you something is wrong before any error appears.

Error rate tells you about failures; freshness tells you about silent stalling.

78 Why alert on absence?Medium

Because the most dangerous integration failure is silence: a feed that normally delivers 5,000 records delivered zero, and nothing errored.

Most monitoring only alerts on errors, which misses exactly this case.

79 What belongs in an integration runbook?Medium

How to check health, where the logs are, how to find a record by correlation id, how to replay a dead letter, who owns the other end, and the credential expiry dates.

Written before the incident, by the person who built it — not reconstructed at 3am.

80 How do you test an integration before go-live?Medium

Unit tests with mocks for your logic, then a smoke suite (Postman via Newman) against a sandbox exercising the real contract and auth, then a volume rehearsal.

And explicitly test the failure paths: timeout, 500, malformed payload, duplicate delivery.

81 The other team's test environment is unreliable.Medium

Build a mock service you control for your own development, and agree scheduled windows for genuine end-to-end testing.

Document the dependency as a project risk — it affects your timeline and it is not a technical problem you can solve.

82 How do you handle an integration incident?Medium

Contain (stop the bleeding — pause the job, open the circuit breaker), assess scope with the logs and reconciliation, repair with replay, then fix forward.

Containment first. Diagnosing while data continues to diverge makes the repair larger.

83 A reconciliation shows 2% divergence every day.Hard

A systematic issue, not noise: a data shape you reject, a mapping gap, a timing boundary, or a filter difference.

Sample the divergent records and look for the pattern. Persistent divergence that gets repaired daily is a bug being masked.

84 How do you monitor a third party's health?Medium

Synthetic checks: a scheduled lightweight call that alerts on failure or slow response, independent of business traffic.

Then you learn about their outage before your users do, and you have evidence for the conversation.

85 Observability costs effort. How do you justify it?Medium

In incident terms: time to detect, time to diagnose, time to repair — with examples from your own last few incidents.

"We could not tell which records were affected" is an argument everyone understands.

86 Should middleware own the retry logic?Medium

Usually yes — it has better primitives than Apex, durable state and no governor limits.

Keep retry in one place. Retry in both Salesforce and middleware produces duplicate attempts nobody predicted.

87 Who should own an integration long-term?Medium

A named team with the runbook, the monitoring and the credential calendar — not "whoever built it".

Unowned integrations are the ones that break silently and take the longest to fix.

88 How do you hand over an integration?Medium

Register entry, contract documentation, runbook, monitoring dashboard, credential expiry dates, and a walkthrough of one real failure.

The failure walkthrough is the part that makes the handover real.

Judgement (89–100)

89 You are handed an integration design to review. Where do you start?Hard

Data ownership, then the latency requirement, then the consistency model and what compensates for failure.

If those four are not answered, the technology choices cannot be evaluated — they are downstream of them.

90 A design has no failure handling.Medium

Ask the four questions: what does the user see, what data is affected, how long until it self-heals, what manual step exists.

Those turn an abstract objection into a concrete gap the team can close.

91 The simplest design is also the least impressive.Medium

Take the simplest design. Nightly batch with reconciliation that runs for five years without incident is better engineering than an elegant event mesh nobody can debug.

Operability is a design quality, and it is the one most often sacrificed.

92 A vendor insists on their integration pattern.Medium

Understand the constraint behind it — often their product genuinely only supports one method.

Then isolate it behind a facade so their pattern does not dictate your architecture, and document the risk if it is poor.

93 How do you estimate integration work?Hard

Separate the contract (usually well understood) from the unknowns: their environment availability, their data quality, their rate limits, and the failure handling.

Price the unknowns as spikes. Integration estimates go wrong on the other team's readiness far more often than on your own code.

94 Two systems have incompatible availability expectations.Medium

Decouple them: a queue or event between the two, so the less available system does not take the other down.

Synchronous coupling means your availability is the product of both systems', which is always worse than either.

95 What is the most common integration design mistake?Hard

Designing only the happy path — no idempotency, no retry classification, no dead letter, no reconciliation — and treating a timeout as a failure.

Followed by accepting "real-time" without decomposing it, and exposing raw sObjects as an API.

96 A stakeholder wants to skip reconciliation to save time.Medium

Explain what it costs: without it, you cannot answer "is the data correct?" and the first divergence becomes an unbounded investigation.

It is usually a day of work. Frame it as the thing that makes the integration defensible.

97 How do you decide what not to integrate?Medium

If no decision or process in Salesforce depends on the data, do not bring it in — link to it, or report on it where it lives.

Every integrated field is permanent cost: mapping, sync, storage, reconciliation and support.

98 An integration has been "temporary" for three years.Medium

Treat it as permanent and give it what permanent things need: an owner, a runbook, monitoring, documentation and a credential calendar.

Or decommission it deliberately. The one thing not to do is continue pretending it is temporary.

99 How do you explain your design to a non-technical sponsor?Medium

In outcomes: how fresh the data will be, what happens when something fails, how you will know, and how long recovery takes.

They do not need the pattern names. They need to know the failure modes are designed for, which is the thing most designs cannot claim.

100 One habit that most improves integration design?Hard

Writing the field-level ownership matrix before anything else. Who owns each field, in which direction it flows, and what happens on conflict.

It is an afternoon of work, it resolves most design arguments, and it exposes the organisational decisions that are the real work hiding behind the technical ones.
